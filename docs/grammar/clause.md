# The clause

How to read and write one Agebon **clause** (roughly, one simple sentence): who does what, how each word is shaped, and the default order.

## Beginner {#beginner}

You already write a [citation](word-endings.md): root + ending (`udozol`, `owurel`, `uzozen`). In a **sentence**, put a **role letter** in front. That first letter says what the word is doing (who acts, the action, and so on). The root and ending stay the ones you already chose; only the first letter changes.

### Who acts and the action

`/z/` marks the **subject** (who acts). `/v/` marks the **verb** (the action). Write **role letter + root + ending** as one word (`zudozol` from the citation `udozol`).

A period ends the statement. With only a subject and a verb, the order is **Subject – Verb**.

> `zudozol vowurel.`
>
> z-dog | v-walk
>
> "A dog walks."

A name you already cite with **-n** (`uzozen`) keeps **-n** in the sentence:

> `zuzozen vowurel.`
>
> z-Uzozen | v-walk
>
> "Uzozen walks."

### Direct object (`/d/`)

The **direct object** is who or what the action is done to. Mark it with `/d/`. The usual order is **Subject – Direct Object – Verb**.

Because the first letter already says each word’s role, you may move those three words without changing who did what or what the sentence means. Moving a word does not stress it either. The usual order is still the one to use, because listeners expect it. Joined verbs are the one exception: there a word's place says which verb it goes with ([verb-phrase forms](join-across-roles.md#vp-clause-forms)).

> `zudozol dagozol vawebol.`
>
> z-dog | d-cat | v-see
>
> "A dog sees a cat."

Same meaning, object first:

> `dagozol zudozol vawebol.`
>
> d-cat | z-dog | v-see
>
> "A dog sees a cat."

### Adjectives (`/ɡ/`) {#adjectives-ɡ}

An **adjective** names a property of a noun (*angry*). Mark it with `/ɡ/` and put it right after the noun it describes. You can stack several adjectives after one noun.

> `zudozol gunemul vowurel.`
>
> [z-dog | g-angry] | v-walk
>
> "An angry dog walks."

When the root names an event, the adjective says the noun is in the middle of it: `gowurel` after *dog* is *a walking dog* ([in progress](predication.md#in-progress)).

### Extra detail on an adjective (`/w/`) {#adjective-detail-w}

To say how strongly a property holds (*very* angry), add a word marked with `/w/` immediately before the adjective. The `/w/` word describes only the adjective right after it, not the noun.

> `zudozol welazom gunemul vowurel.`
>
> [z-dog | [w-very | g-angry]] | v-walk
>
> "A very angry dog walks."

### Adverbs (`/h/`) {#adverbs-h}

An **adverb** says how, when, or where an action happens. Mark it with `/h/`. You may place it anywhere in the sentence, and several adverbs can sit next to each other (`hedurem halozum` *hastily and quietly*). With joined verbs, its place says which verb it describes ([verb-phrase forms](join-across-roles.md#vp-clause-forms)).

> `zudozol hedurem vowurel.`
>
> z-dog | h-haste | v-walk
>
> "A dog walks hastily."

### Stance (`/th/`) {#stance-th}

An adverb describes the action. A **stance** word describes how **you**, the speaker, stand toward the whole claim: how sure you are, how you know it, why it holds, or whether it is only imagined. Mark it with `/th/`. Like `/h/`, it may sit anywhere in the sentence. `th` counts as one letter ([phonology](phonology.md#consonants)).

> `zudozol thevemom vowurel.`
>
> z-dog | th-doubt | v-walk
>
> "I doubt a dog is walking."

The adverb describes the walking. The stance word describes your claim about it.

A stance is yours **now**, as you speak.

`/th/` is for what is in a mind. With nothing else marked, that mind is yours.

### Intention adverbs {#intention-adverbs}

Some `/h/` words say something about the doer that others could check: that they are [trying](intention.md#try) it, [can](intention.md#ability-fallback) do it, or that the one it happens to [agreed](sakes.md#consent). These are **intention adverbs**. Two more `/h/` words keep the books on an outcome: whether it [still counts](knowing.md#residue-and-former-climate). Unlike *hastily*, each of these covers the **whole clause** wherever it sits, the way a stance does. A [plan](intention.md#plan) or a [decision](intention.md#decision-revisability) is in a mind, so it is a `/th/` stance instead.

An ability does not say the act happens: *can walk* is not *walks*. A try and a consent do say it happens.

### Extra nouns (`/b/`) {#extra-nouns}

`/b/` marks an **extra noun**: a person or thing beyond the subject and direct object. It has two jobs. You tell them apart by the word **immediately before** the `/b/` word.

**Hosted: completing a relation.** Some words name a relation that needs a second person or thing (*like …*: like whom?). Put the relation word in `/ɡ/`, `/h/`, or `/th/`, then put the other noun in `/b/` right after it. The relation word is then the `/b/` word’s **host**. (cue: 🪞 *mirror*: the image is *like* the model.)

The listener goes by position, not meaning: a `/b/` word right after any `/ɡ/`, `/h/`, or `/th/` word completes that word, even a word that names no relation. It is never read as the recipient described below, so do not put a recipient there.

> `hewom buzozen`
>
> [h-like | b-Uzozen]
>
> "like Uzozen"

A `/w/` word still goes before the host, so the host and its `/b/` stay side by side.

> `welazom hewom buzozen`
>
> [[w-very | h-like] | b-Uzozen]
>
> "very like Uzozen"

> `zudozol vezuvol hewom buzozen.`
>
> z-dog | v-sleep | [h-like | b-Uzozen]
>
> "A dog sleeps like Uzozen."

**Unhosted: who receives.** A `/b/` word anywhere else is the person who receives what the verb passes on: who gets the thing given, or who hears what is told. What is given or said is still `/d/`. Each verb has at most one of these. Its usual place is right after the verb: **Subject – Direct Object – Verb – Extra noun**.

> `zuzozen vezuyel bolazun.`
>
> z-Uzozen | v-tell | b-Olazun
>
> "Uzozen tells Olazun."

**Compare with:** `hewom bolazun` is *like Olazun*, because a `/b/` right after `/h/` completes it. The usual place after the verb keeps the person who receives clear of any `/ɡ/`, `/h/`, or `/th/` word. If you move it elsewhere, make sure it does not come right after one: `bolazun hedurem`, or after a finished pair (`hewom buzozen bolazun`).

### Role letters {#role-letters}

The role letters you have used so far:

| Agebon | Use | Cue |
|--------|-----|-----|
| `/z/` | subject (who acts) | **z** ≈ star (who the sentence is about) |
| `/d/` | direct object (who is acted on) | **d** ≈ done to (sound of *acted on*) |
| `/b/` | extra noun: recipient of the verb, or the piece after a relation word | **b** ≈ bolted on (the extra piece) |
| `/v/` | verb (the action) | **v** ≈ *verb* (the English name for the job starts with it) |
| `/ɡ/` | adjective (a property of the noun) | **ɡ** ≈ grade (a rating of the noun) |
| `/w/` | extra detail on the next `/ɡ/`, `/h/`, or `/th/` word | **w** ≈ with (stuck to that host) |
| `/h/` | adverb (how / when / where) | **h** starts *how* / *when* / *where* |
| `/th/` | stance (how sure, how known, why, as if): what is in a mind | **th** ≈ *think* (your stance on the claim) |

### Adjectives before the noun (`gl-`) {#left-bound-adjectives}

English puts the adjective before the noun (*an angry dog*). Agebon can too: add **`l`** as the second letter (`/ɡ/` + **l** + root + ending), and that adjective describes the **next** noun instead of the one before it. This lets you choose the order for meter or emphasis, and it frees the spot after the noun for another adjective. (cue: **l** ≈ lean: the word leans toward the noun ahead.)

> `glunemul zudozol.`
>
> [gl-angry | z-dog]
>
> "an angry dog"

A `/b/` word still follows that adjective, and a `/w/` word still sits immediately before it (`welazom glunemul zudozol` = *a very angry dog*). This extra **`l`** goes only on adjectives (`/ɡ/`). A noun must come after it: with no noun to lean on, write a plain `/ɡ/` adjective after the noun instead (`zudozol gunemul`).

A [family name](word-endings.md#multipart-names) is a `/ɡ/` word too, so **`gl-`** puts it first, for a culture that names people family first. It leans on a [call](speech-moves.md#vocative) the same way.

> `glalowen zalen vowurel.`
>
> [gl-Alowen | z-Alen] | v-walk
>
> "Alowen Alen walks."

> `glalowen yalen.`
>
> [gl-Alowen | y-Alen]
>
> "Alowen Alen!" (calling Alowen Alen)

### Complex chaining {#complex-chaining}

A relation adjective and its hosted `/b/` (*the same as Uzozen*: **`gorol`** + `/b/`) work as one unit after a noun. A `/w/` word that grades the relation goes immediately before the `/ɡ/` word, as usual. You can keep chaining: a plain adjective after that pair describes the **extra noun**, not the first noun. (cue: 🪙 *coin*: two faces of one thing.)

> `zagayul welazom gorol buzozen.`
>
> z-guard | [[w-very | g-SAME] | b-Uzozen]
>
> "a guard who is the very same person as Uzozen"

> `zagayul gorol buzozen guzemol.`
>
> z-guard | [g-SAME | [b-Uzozen | g-sleepy]]
>
> "a guard who is the same person as sleepy Uzozen"

The same holds after an `/h/` or `/th/` host: an adjective after its `/b/` describes that extra noun.

> `zuzozen vezuvol hewom budozol godamul.`
>
> z-Uzozen | v-sleep | [h-like | [b-dog | g-tired]]
>
> "Uzozen sleeps like a tired dog."

Because a second relation after the pair would attach to the extra noun, you cannot put two relation adjectives **after** the same first noun. To keep both on that noun in one clause, put one [before the noun](#left-bound-adjectives) with **`gl-`**; its `/b/` still follows it. Otherwise use [another sentence](dependents.md#which-noun).

> `glorol buzozen zagayul gorol bobayel.`
>
> [[gl-SAME | b-Uzozen] | z-guard] | [g-SAME | b-police]
>
> "a guard who is both Uzozen and the police officer"

A verb can take several `/h/` or `/th/` units. Each plain word counts as one unit, and so does a host with its `/b/` (plus any `/w/` before it).

**Compare with:** a [hook](hooks.md#extra-noun) followed by `/b/` means *in* / *at* / *for*, not the person who receives.

### How much: degree words on `/w/` {#degree-w}

Beginner used **`welazom`** *very* before an adjective. The same `/w/` slot takes a few stock degree words, and each one works before an adverb as well as an adjective.

<!-- cheat-sheet: word-shape-clause -->
| Agebon | English | Cue |
|--------|---------|-----|
| `welazom` | *very* | 🐘: big, as a degree |
| `wohalal` | *extremely* / *overwhelmingly* | 🌊: an ocean of it |
| `wamezom` | *slightly* / *a bit* | 🐁: small, as a degree |
| `wadowom` | *quite* / *fairly* | 🔉: medium volume, not loud or soft |
| `wuhuram` | *completely* / *totally* / *entirely* / *fully* | 💯: a hundred is the whole |

> `zudozol welazom hedurem vowurel.`
>
> z-dog | [w-very | h-haste] | v-walk
>
> "A dog walks very hastily."

> `zudozol wohalal gunemul vowurel.`
>
> [z-dog | [w-ocean | g-angry]] | v-walk
>
> "An extremely angry dog walks."

> `zudozol wamezom gunemul vowurel.`
>
> [z-dog | [w-small | g-angry]] | v-walk
>
> "A slightly angry dog walks."

> `zudozol wadowom gunemul vowurel.`
>
> [z-dog | [w-quite | g-angry]] | v-walk
>
> "A fairly angry dog walks."

The same words also go right before a reaction, to say how strongly it holds ([describing a reaction](speech-moves.md#describe-turn-word)).

Two stock words go in `/h/` and describe the verb itself. Use **`hoyurem`** *kind of* / *sort of* to hedge the action, and **`hurugem`** for *again*.

<!-- cheat-sheet: word-shape-clause -->
| Agebon | English | Cue |
|--------|---------|-----|
| `hoyurem` | *kind of* / *sort of* | 📈: some degree, not a full one |
| `hurugem` | *again* | 🔁: the same thing once more |

> `zuzozen hoyurem vowurel.`
>
> z-Uzozen | h-kind-of | v-walk
>
> "Uzozen kind of walks."

> `zudozol hurugem varuvel.`
>
> z-dog | h-again | v-run
>
> "The dog runs again."

### Leaving out who acts {#no-subject}

English uses the passive (*The cat was seen*, *Mistakes were made*) to avoid naming who acted. Agebon simply leaves out the `/z/` word. The verb and its object stay the same, and the sentence says nothing about who did it.

> `duzozen vawebol.`
>
> d-Uzozen | v-see
>
> "Uzozen was seen."

**Compare with:** [`zumon`](pronouns.md#special-pronouns) *someone* says that somebody did it, without saying who. With no subject at all, the sentence does not mention a doer. A missing subject never means the [topic](pronouns.md#topic-pronoun): to make the topic the doer, write `zalon`.

## Advanced {#advanced}

### English emphasis and clefts {#english-emphasis}

English often moves or stresses a word to single it out: *It's a cat that Uzozen sees*, *It was Uzozen who saw the cat*, or a stressed *Uzozen saw the cat*. Agebon word order carries no emphasis. A sentence's usual order is set by its [speech act](speech-moves.md#act-word-order), and any other order means exactly the same (with joined verbs, a word's place says which verb it goes with, [verb-phrase forms](join-across-roles.md#vp-clause-forms)). When you translate, ask what the English emphasis is doing.

If the English sets one person or thing against others (*Uzozen, not someone else*), put the [contrastive focus](speech-moves.md#tone-marks) mark **`&`** on that word.

> `zuzozen &dagozol vawebol.`
>
> z-Uzozen | &d-cat | v-see
>
> "It's a cat that Uzozen sees." (not something else)

If the English only puts the new part first, with no others to set it against (answering *What does Uzozen see?* with *It's a cat that Uzozen sees*), write the plain sentence. What came before already shows which part is new.

> `zuzozen dagozol vawebol.`
>
> z-Uzozen | d-cat | v-see
>
> "Uzozen sees a cat."

Translating the other way, an Agebon sentence in an unusual order (a line fitted to a tune, say) becomes a plain English sentence.

<!-- cheat-sheet: word-shape-clause -->
| Agebon | Use | English |
|--------|-----|---------|
| `zuzozen dagozol vawebol.` | plain | *Uzozen sees a cat.* / *It's a cat that Uzozen sees.* (answering *what?*) |
| `&zuzozen dagozol vawebol.` | subject contrasted | *It was Uzozen who saw the cat.* |
| `zuzozen &dagozol vawebol.` | object contrasted | *It's a cat that Uzozen sees.* (not something else) |
| `zuzozen dagozol &vawebol.` | act contrasted | *Uzozen sees the cat.* (rather than doing something else to it) |

**Compare with:** what the whole conversation is about is the [topic](pronouns.md#topic), set only by an `/x/` word.

### Starting a sentence audibly {#audible-start}

In writing, the period shows where one sentence ends and the next begins. In speech, a listener may miss that pause, especially in singing or with free word order. When that could happen, begin the next sentence with a word that can only start one: *yes* / *no*, the name of the person you address, or the statement word `yal`.

#### English → Agebon {#advanced-english-to-agebon}

**1.** *Hastily, Uzozen punches a balloon.*

::: details Show answer
`hedurem zuzozen daboval vanuvul.`

h-haste | z-Uzozen | d-balloon | v-punch
:::

**2.** *What does Olazun punch?* *It's a drum that Olazun punches.*

::: details Show answer
`zolazun dudoral vanuvul.`

z-Olazun | d-drum | v-punch

Nothing is set against the drum, so the plain sentence is enough.
:::

**3.** *It's a drum, not a trumpet, that Olazun punches.*

::: details Show answer
`zolazun &dudoral vanuvul.`

z-Olazun | &d-drum | v-punch
:::

**4.** *It was Ahelan, not someone else, who saw the mask.*

::: details Show answer

#### What changes {#advanced-what-changes}

**1.** `zuzozen daboval vawebol.` / `&zuzozen daboval vawebol.`

::: details Show answer
z-Uzozen | d-balloon | v-see

&z-Uzozen | d-balloon | v-see

The first is plain *Uzozen sees a balloon*; the second stresses Uzozen: *it was Uzozen, not someone else*.
:::

**2.** `zahelan dudoral vawebol.` / `zahelan dudoral &vawebol.`

::: details Show answer
z-Ahelan | d-drum | v-see

z-Ahelan | d-drum | &v-see

The second contrasts the act: Ahelan *sees* the drum, as opposed to doing something else to it.
:::
