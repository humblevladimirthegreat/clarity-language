# Ageben Introduction

**Ageben** (`agebe` + the name ending **-n**) translates to English *clarity*.

Ageben has two kinds of goals: psychological goals, which say what the language is for, and structural goals, which shape how it is built. This page covers the structural goals and how to work through these docs.

## Purpose {#purpose}

Ageben's psychological goals are compassion, rationality, and empowerment. [Why Ageben](purpose.md) explains those goals, tours the features that serve them, and says what the language does not claim.

If you want an easy start before the grammar, [Claritish](claritish/index.md) teaches a few Ageben words you can drop into English today.

## Structural goals {#structural-goals}

Three structural goals shape how the sounds, words, and grammar are built:

- **Unambiguous but usable:** every sentence has exactly one reading, precise enough for a program to understand, without making ordinary speech hard to say.
- **Singable:** syllable shapes are chosen to be easy to sing.
- **Reusable:** a small set of patterns does many jobs. The same few endings, the same vowel series, and the same scales return across the grammar, so each pattern you learn keeps paying off on later pages.

The first goal shows up in three places: in how a sentence is built, in what a pronoun points to, and in what a word means.

### One role per word {#syntactic}

In a clause, every content word (a word built on a root, such as a noun, verb, or adjective) begins with a **role letter**: a letter that names the word's role in the clause (subject, object, verb, …). English often makes you work out a word's role from its position; in Ageben you read it off the first letter. The default order is subject, object, verb, but you may put the words in any order, because each word still carries its role.

### One thing per pronoun {#referential}

English *it* and *they* can point to almost anything mentioned earlier. An Ageben pronoun copies the stem of an earlier word (the word without its role letter and ending) and points to the most recently mentioned word with that stem, so a fixed rule picks the one thing it means.

The other pronouns follow rules that are just as fixed. A **role pointer** names someone by the part they played in a recent event (*the latest doer*, *the other one*). A few special pronouns cover the speaker, the listener, and similar roles. To keep several people or things apart, you can give each a **tag**, a short label (A, E, O, or U) that later pronouns reuse. When the talk is about one thing, you can name it as the **topic**, and one pronoun always means it.

### One sense per entry {#semantic}

Each dictionary entry has one sense. When a meaning is related to a word but belongs to a different area of life, it gets its own compound instead of becoming a second sense: the area first, then the kind. For example, *love* in the *crush* sense is its own compound, not a second meaning of *love*. The [compound pages](/compounds) teach how to spell these.

### Tools those goals make possible
<a id="tools"></a>

Because of role letters, a small fixed set of endings, and spelling that matches sound, a program can read a word's role, what a pronoun points to, and how to pronounce it straight from the writing. You write nothing extra for the tools: they read the same sentences people do.

- **[Inspect](inspect.md):** paste a sentence and click or highlight a word to see its role, its root's sense, and how the clause fits together.
- **Pronunciation:** the same page shows an **IPA:** line, which writes out the text as spoken in the International Phonetic Alphabet (a standard notation for speech sounds). Since each letter has one sound and syllables come in only a few shapes, pronunciation follows directly from the spelling.

## How to learn from these docs
<a id="how-to-learn"></a>

Grammar pages are grouped in the sidebar as **Beginner**, **Intermediate**, and **Advanced**. Finish every page in the Beginner group and take its review, then do the same for Intermediate, then Advanced.

Start with [Phonology](/phonology) for letters and word edges, then [Word endings](/word-endings) for citation endings (by then you can already say your name as a [greeting](/word-endings#greeting)), then [The clause](/clause) for clause shape, [Speech moves](/speech-moves) for turns, and [Dependents](/dependents).

The rest of the sidebar is for looking things up:

| In the sidebar | What it is | When to open it |
|----------------|------------|-----------------|
| **Review**, last in each level ([Beginner](/review), [Intermediate](/review-intermediate), [Advanced](/review-advanced)) | One item per page at that level. Each answer links back to the section it tests | When you finish a level |
| **Tools**: [Terminology](terminology.md) | The English names these pages use for grammar, each with a short gloss and a link to the section that teaches it | When a grammar term has slipped your mind |
| **Tools**: [Lexicon](lexicon.md) | Roots and dictionary compounds, and a way to respell an English name | When you need a word |
| **Tools**: [Inspect](inspect.md) | A word-by-word breakdown of a sentence | When you want to check a sentence |
| **Cheat Sheets** | A printable lookup page for each part of the grammar. | **[Saying it in Ageben](english.md)** | Answers to English tasks (*because*, *by Friday*, *walked*), using forms taught on the grammar pages | After those pages, in any order |

### Table columns: Ageben, Use, English, Same root as, and Cue
<a id="cues"></a>

Inventory tables on grammar pages use these columns:

| Column | What it is |
|--------|------------|
| **Ageben** | The word or letter you write. Normally this is the citation form (how the word is written on its own, outside a sentence). When **Same root as** is filled in, it is the spelling used inside a sentence, with its role letter. |
| **Use** | What the form does: its job in the sentence, such as subject, question, or *because* joining two sentences. This is the rule. |
| **English** | What you would say: the sense to produce or understand. In translation practice, a person's English is their name (*Azozon*), not the virtue word the name is built from. |
| **Same root as** | The everyday kind of the same root, written as a citation (ending **-l**). It is filled in when the row's English is a different word from the root's own English: the *wall* root on a row whose English is *because*, or the *eye* root on a row for *see*. It is blank when the two match, and not every table has this column. |
| **Cue** | A memory aid for the letter, vowel, or picture behind the row. Optional. |

In a cue, `≈` means "sounds like." When **Same root as** is filled in, **Cue** does not repeat the everyday English; it only says why that picture fits.

Above each **Practice** checkpoint is a word bank with two tables. **New words** (English, Ageben, Cue) lists the words that checkpoint introduces; when **Ageben** is not the citation of the English, the cue names the citation's sense (👁️ from *eye*). **Review** (English, Ageben) lists words from earlier checkpoints.
