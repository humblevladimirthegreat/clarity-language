# Why Ageben

What Ageben is for, what it looks like, a tour of the features that serve its aims, and what it does not claim.

## Purpose {#purpose}

Ageben builds psychological concepts and self-improvement techniques into its vocabulary and grammar, with the intent that using the language nudges you toward healthier habits of thought.

Its psychological goals are three outcomes: [compassion](#compassion), [rationality](#rationality), and [empowerment](#empowerment). [Psychological features](#psychological-features) describes each one with the features that serve it. Ageben also has [structural goals](introduction.md#structural-goals): every sentence has exactly one reading, the sounds are easy to sing, and a few patterns do many jobs.

Ageben is not therapy, and its effects are a design intent, not a proven result. The language is in beta ([limits](#limits)).

## Why language can help {#why-language-can-help}

Unhelpful thoughts are often automatic: catastrophizing a small setback into *my life is ruined*, treating two options as the only ones, or calling a feeling *anger* without asking what need is behind it. Healthier framings exist, but they are hard to reach in the moment.

Ageben puts those framings into ordinary words and into optional pieces added to words. When you speak or think in the language, the helpful distinction is already part of the sentence, where you can choose it.

## Ageben at a glance {#at-a-glance}

Here is a common ruminating thought in English: *I failed. I'm no good at this, and I never will be.* English lets you say all of that without checking any of it. Here is how the same thought might come out in Ageben:

> `zoguvom thaber guwathum. zuwen veveroxem.`
>
> /zo̞.ɡʉ.vo̞m ðä.be̞ɹ ɡʉ.wä.ðʉm. zʉ.we̞n ve̞.ve̞.ɹe̞.ʒo̞m/
>
> z-failure | th-INTUITION.weak | g-competence-unmet-modifiable . z-speaker | v-triumph-unable-modifiable
>
> "It's only a faint hunch, but my failure set back my competence, in a way that can change. I can't succeed yet."

That is five words, and three of them make you choose something English lets you skip. Each choice is a question about the thought itself, and answering it honestly is what turned *never* into *yet*:

- `thaber` says how you know: a gut sense, and only a faint one. Other words say you saw it, remember it, worked it out from clues, or were told, and other endings say the hunch is strong. Said once, it covers the next sentence too ([evidentiality](/evidentiality#evidentiality)).
- `guwathum` names what the failure costs: competence, one of the [sakes](/sakes) (things that are good for a person). Its ending says the cost can change with effort or circumstance; other endings say it is lasting, or only for now. Other sakes, such as relatedness or purpose, name a different cost, and swapping the *unmet* vowel for the *met* one names a gain instead. The sake word also makes the failure yours, with no separate word for *my*.
- `veveroxem` is *can't succeed*, but only *yet*: it may still change. Other vowels say *right now* (you usually can) or *never* ([ability](/can-and-cant#ability)).

Ageben does not force the gentler answer. You can still say *I can never succeed* (`zuwen veveroxum.`), call the hunch strong, or call the cost lasting, and sometimes that is true. But English *I'll never be good at this* does not ask how you know or how lasting it is, so the harsh version slips by unchecked. In Ageben you cannot say *can't* without choosing among *right now*, *yet*, and *never*, and that moment of choosing is a chance to notice when *yet* is the honest answer.

The first letter of each word tells you its role in the sentence: `/z/` the subject, `/ɡ/` what the subject is, `/v/` the verb, `/th/` what is in your mind. Because the roles live on the letters, word order is free, and every sentence still has exactly one reading. [Inspect](inspect.md) opens with this sentence loaded, so you can click through each word.

## Try it in English first {#try-it-in-english}

You don't have to learn the whole language to start using these ideas. [Claritish](claritish/index.md) is a set of short lessons on dropping single Ageben words into everyday English. For example, English *I'm anxious* can mean anxious right now or an anxious person. *I'm `anxious-tha`* says it is this stretch, not who you are, where English would need a whole extra clause. Other lessons cover how you know, how far a label reaches, thanks that name the need, and more.

Every word Claritish teaches is already real Ageben, so nothing you learn there is wasted.

## Psychological features {#psychological-features}

This section tours how the three aims show up in vocabulary and grammar. Under each aim, the features are grouped by the thinking trap they answer. Each feature says what English blurs, what Ageben marks instead, and what that helps you do; the linked pages teach the forms. A group's name is the habit its features push against, not a bias they have been shown to cure ([limits](#limits)).

### Compassion {#compassion}

Compassion here means accepting yourself and other people, and seeing a judgment as a judgment when you make one. The features under it keep a hard moment, a feeling, or a cause from turning into a verdict on a person.

#### Treating a moment as identity {#trap-identity}

A hard moment, a label, or a past pattern hardens into a fixed fact about a person. The features here are meant to keep a bad stretch a stretch.

##### How far a label reaches {#how-far-a-label-reaches}

English *Alazun is angry* can mean one outburst or the kind of person Alazun is. One act slides into a verdict on someone's nature, and a name for a problem (*lazy*, *anxious*) starts to sound like its cause.

[Label reach](/label-seams#label-reach) lets any label say what kind of claim it makes: about this stretch of doing, about a practiced role, about one relationship (*a stranger to Azozon*), or about a type that follows the person across scenes.

Marking a label's reach helps you describe what happened without turning it into a verdict on who someone is, yourself included.

In English: [Claritish: Labels](claritish/labels.md).

##### Can, can't, and won't {#can-cant-and-wont}

English *I can't* can mean a temporary block, a lasting inability, or a choice not to.

[Ability](/can-and-cant#ability) says whether someone can do an activity or have a property, and for *can't*, which kind: not right now, not now but it may change, or not ever. Choosing not to is an ordinary *won't*, not a kind of *can't*.

Saying *can't right now* or *may yet change* instead of *impossible* helps you stop treating a temporary block as a fixed incapability.

In English: [Claritish: Can and can't](claritish/can-and-cant.md).

##### What still counts, and what used to be true {#what-still-counts}

English *has done* and *used to* mix two questions: when something happened, and whether it still matters now. *Used to* also leaves open whether a habit still describes you today.

[Residue](/residue-and-former#residue) says the result of a past event still counts now, like a debt still unpaid or a door still shut. A [former pattern](/residue-and-former#former-climate) says something was usual without claiming it is how things are now. Both are measured from a moment you can name (*as of Friday*, [whose now](/as-of#as-of)), and neither is a past tense.

Because residue is marked, an event you mention without it counts as closed, so carrying a past event forward is a choice and not a default. Marking a former pattern lets *I was always this way* describe the past without claiming the present, and naming the moment lets you weigh an old situation by its own date instead of against today.

#### Treating a feeling as a label {#trap-feeling}

A feeling, a thanks, or a *should* arrives as a single opaque label, so what is met or unmet and what would help stay out of view.

##### Sakes, gratitude, and oughts {#sakes-gratitude-and-oughts}

English often blurs *I want*, *I need*, *I should*, and *this is good for me*, so gratitude, motive, and ought run together.

Ageben's [sakes](/sakes) are a fixed list: autonomy, competence, understanding, purpose, relatedness, beneficence, pleasure, physical, or unspecified. Each sake word also says how you stand toward that sake:

| Stance | Meaning |
| ------ | ------- |
| Met | This serves the sake (gratitude, savoring) |
| Motive | You act for this sake |
| Prescription | This act ought to be done for this sake |
| Unmet | This costs the sake |

Naming the sake and your stance toward it helps you notice gratitude, motive, and oughts instead of blurring them into a vague *want*, *should*, or *good*.

In English: [Claritish: Oughts and motives](claritish/oughts-and-motives.md).

##### Emotions as composition {#emotions-as-composition}

Opaque labels (*angry*, *anxious*, *proud*) hide which sake is in play and what to ask for.

Ageben [builds a feeling word](/how-you-feel#feeling-word) from three parts: the need (a sake, and whether it is met or unmet), the urge (what the feeling pulls you to do, such as stay, act on someone, or move away), and how long the feeling lasts (settled in, or a wave that will pass).

Building the feeling from those parts shows you what to ask for, instead of leaving you stuck at the label.

In English: [Claritish: What a feeling wants](claritish/what-a-feeling-wants.md).

##### Thanks that name the need {#thanks-that-name-the-need}

English *thank you* says you are grateful, but not for what.

Ageben has no set phrase for [thanks](/thanks-and-sorry#thanks-sorry). You say which of your sakes was met, and whether the payoff lasts or is only for now; saying that to the person who acted is the thanks. The same word, said about a thing you own, appreciates what you already have ([personal possession](/sakes#personal-possession)).

Naming the need that was met makes thanks specific, which helps both of you notice what actually helped.

In English: [Claritish: Thanks and sorry](claritish/thanks-and-sorry.md).

#### Treating a cause as a fault {#trap-fault}

An explanation of what made something happen slides into blame, and one cause among several ends up carrying the whole outcome.

##### Cause, blame, and share {#cause-blame-and-share}

English *because of you* can mean you made it happen, you did something wrong, or you were one part of it.

The ending on a [because](/fault#fault) word says which claim you make: plain cause with no fault, a reason that broke a norm (*it's their fault*), or one share among other causes (*partly because*). Plain cause is the default.

Keeping cause apart from fault helps you explain what happened without blaming, and saying *partly* keeps one person, including you, from carrying a whole outcome.

### Rationality {#rationality}

Rationality here means seeing what a claim rests on, so that the common biases that make self-delusion easy are harder to miss. The features under it show the grounds, the kind of link, and the options behind what you say.

#### Treating a guess as fact {#trap-certainty}

A guess, a rumor, a hazy memory, or a wish gets treated as settled fact, and a few cases get treated as a rule. Forecasts belong here too, and they are described with plans and decisions under [wanting, planning, and deciding](#wanting-planning-and-deciding).

##### How you know, and *could be* {#how-you-know-and-could-be}

It is easy to treat a *could be* that keeps looping in your head as settled fact, and to lump memory, hearsay, gut feel, and *maybe they left* together as if they were equally good grounds.

[How you know](/evidentiality#evidentiality) lets a claim about the world name what it rests on: a live look, memory, a pattern, clues, a report, a [deduction](/evidentiality#deduction), or a gut sense. The [ending](/evidentiality#evidence-strength) on that word says how strong the evidence is (a vivid memory or a hazy one, a reliable source or a rumor), separately from how likely the claim is. A separate word, [could be](/knowing#may), says something is possible without claiming that it is.

Marking how you know helps you catch overconfidence and hearsay before a claim hardens into settled fact. Keeping a deduction apart from strong clues helps you catch a leap dressed as a proof, and saying *could be* keeps a guess a guess.

In English: [Claritish: How sure are you?](claritish/could-be.md) and [How do you know?](claritish/how-you-know.md).

##### Always, usually, and how many cases {#always-usually-how-many-cases}

English *always* and *never* sound absolute even when you mean *usually*, and they do not say how many cases they rest on.

A [general claim](/usually-vs-must#generalization) says in its ending whether it allows exceptions: a closed *always* allows none, and an open *always* leaves them open, like *usually* or *as a rule*. When the claim rests on a pattern, the word for how you know says how much pattern there is, from a well-established one to only a few cases.

Saying *usually* when you mean it, and *from a few cases* when that is all you have, helps you catch overgeneralizing before one bad day becomes *it always goes wrong*.

#### Treating a wish as a law {#trap-wish-as-law}

A link between events, a habit, or an ought gets treated as a law the world must obey.

##### Causes and *if* {#causes-and-if}

Everyday English *because*, *leads to*, and *have to* blur three distinctions: a condition that is enough vs one that is required, a reason in the world vs your grounds for saying something, and a wish vs a law of nature.

[Causation](/causation) gives each link its own word: *if*, *only if*, *because*, and *if and only if*. An intended result ([so that](/dependents#so-that)) has a separate word, which names your aim for the action without claiming the outcome happens. When the "rule" is really an ought (*hard work should earn promotion*), you say it as a sakes [prescription](/ought-for-a-sake#sake-force) on the act, not with a causation word.

Keeping these apart helps you stop treating wishes and habits as laws the world must obey.

#### Treating a few options as all of them {#trap-frames}

Two options pose as the only ones, and an unstated standard decides the ranking.

##### Open lists, closed lists, and false dichotomies {#open-and-closed-lists}

It is easy to present two options as if they were the only ones possible.

[Joins](/joins) mark what kind of list you are building (inventory, exclusive choice, inclusive *and/or*, rank, …) and whether the listed items are the only ones in play (a closed list) or more could be added (an open list).

Saying whether a list is open or closed helps you notice false dichotomies, and leaves room for options you have not named yet.

##### The standard behind a comparison {#judgment-bars}

English *worse*, *better*, and *not good enough* can rank options against a standard nobody has said out loud.

A comparison like *worse than…* names its [bar](/bars#bars), the standard it measures against: *than usual*, *than I expected*, *than you demand*.

Naming the bar keeps the standard visible and revisable, including when it is only your own expectation.

### Empowerment {#empowerment}

Empowerment here means authentic choice: acting for reasons you can see. The features under it show where a demand comes from, how firm a plan or a yes is, and how much effort a task needs.

#### Treating a choice as forced {#trap-no-choice}

Wanting, needing, and being obliged blur into a bare *should*, and a plan or a pick sounds locked. [Sakes](#sakes-gratitude-and-oughts) already name the reason behind an action, so the features here cover the rest.

##### Who says you must {#who-says-you-must}

English *must*, *have to*, and *is supposed to* sound equally binding, whether a law requires something, a person demands it, or people only expect it. *May* hides the same split for permission.

A [say word](/permission-consent#say-words) says where a *must* comes from: a rule, a person who demands it, only an unwritten expectation that nobody actually demands, or you yourself. It does the same for *may*: a rule allows it, someone said yes, or nobody objected.

Naming the source helps you notice when a *have to* is only an expectation, so you can decide whether to meet it.

In English: [Claritish: Allowed and required](claritish/allowed-required-agreed.md).

##### Wanting, planning, and deciding {#wanting-planning-and-deciding}

English *will* mixes plan and prediction, and *I want to* can sound like a promise. It is easy to talk as if the future were already known, and a pick often sounds more locked than it is.

Ageben splits them: a [want](/want-and-decide#want) is what you wish for, without meaning to do it yet; a [plan](/intention#plan-predict) is what you mean to do; a [decision](/want-and-decide#decision) says how revisable a pick is; and a [forecast](/evidentiality#forecast) is what the world will do. A forecast has no word of its own: you say how you know and add a word for *later*, so every forecast says what it rests on. Someone else's want, plan, or decision takes a [holder](/holder#holder), a word that says how you know what is in their mind.

Splitting these helps you hold a want without owing it to anyone, avoid treating a wishful forecast as settled fact or as a commitment, and keep a revisable pick from sounding locked forever. Marking how you know someone else's intention keeps a guess about what they mean to do from passing as fact.

In English: [Claritish: Wants and plans](claritish/wants-and-plans.md) and [Decisions and tries](claritish/decisions-and-tries.md).

#### Treating effort as all or nothing {#trap-all-or-nothing}

Every attempt can feel like a full commitment that either succeeds or fails, every plan like it has to be complete before you start, and every result like it has to be perfect.

##### Trying it out {#trying-it-out}

English *try* does not say whether you are committing to keep going or only testing whether something works, so every attempt can sound like it will end in success or failure.

[Try](/try#try) says that an attempt happens and leaves the outcome open. Its ending says how far the attempt will go: a committed attempt keeps going until it works or is ruled out, and a trial run is a low-stakes probe where failing is useful to know.

Calling an attempt a trial run helps you start without betting everything on it: if it fails, finding that out was the point.

In English: [Claritish: Decisions and tries](claritish/decisions-and-tries.md).

##### Planning the backup first {#planning-the-backup-first}

A plan made in English often names only the first choice, and it either stays vague or feels like it has to be complete before you act.

A [fallback](/try#fallback) joins an attempt to its backup: *try A; failing that, B*. The backup counts only if the attempt before it fails. The ending on a [plan](/intention#plan-predict) says how much of it is drawn: backups included, steps filled in, or only a direction.

Naming the backup before you start helps a setback turn into the next step instead of a dead end. No level of detail is better than another, so matching the detail to the task keeps planning from becoming a reason not to start.

##### Good enough {#good-enough}

English *enough* and *too much* often compare a result with an unstated ideal, so nothing ever counts as done.

A [sake bar](/bars#sake-bars) measures an amount against what a sake actually needs: *enough*, *too much*, or *not enough* for that need. When you do not name which sake, the unspecified sake stands in.

Measuring against the need instead of against perfect helps you notice when a result is good enough to stop.

#### Treating silence as a yes {#trap-terms}

An act that touches someone else's body, things, or time can go ahead on a yes nobody said, and a yes once given can sound permanent.

##### Consent: whose yes, and how firm {#consent}

English *she's fine with it* does not say whether she signed on, said yes and can take it back, or was never asked.

A [say word](/permission-consent#say-words) names whose yes it is (the one the act happens to, the one it is told or given to, or the doer) and says how binding it is: a binding agreement made in advance, a yes they can take back at any time, or a yes you are only assuming because nobody objected.

Marking an assumed yes as an assumption helps you check before acting, and marking a spoken yes as revocable keeps it theirs to withdraw.

##### How long you can talk {#how-long-you-can-talk}

English *hi* does not say whether you can stay a while, have only a few minutes, have one thing to ask, or are just passing by.

A [greeting](/word-endings#greeting) is your own name said as a sentence. A [conversation-length bid](/conversation-length#conversation-length) adds one vowel to your name: open-ended, a few minutes, one thing to ask, or passing by. The other person can answer with their own bid, and the shorter bid sets the limit for both of you.

Marking the opening that way lets you agree on how long to talk, so saying hello does not commit you to unlimited time.

### Conceptual metaphors {#conceptual-metaphors}

Abstract ideas are hard to hold onto, and without a concrete picture the useful reading of a word can slip away in the moment. This cuts across all the groups above.

Many roots have an abstract sense beside a concrete one, so an idea you cannot see is tied to a familiar scene that already carries the caution. For example:

- [Memory as fishing](/evidentiality#evidentiality) ([a guess as fact](#trap-certainty)): you cast into opaque water and may pull up a fish or a boot, so the memory can be wrong.
- [Live look as attesting](/knowing#live-vs-memory) ([a guess as fact](#trap-certainty)): you report what you see while the scene is still in view.
- [Residue as unpaid debt](/residue-and-former#residue) ([a moment as identity](#trap-identity)): the result still counts, like a balance still owed.
- [A former pattern as an empty nest](/residue-and-former#former-climate) ([a moment as identity](#trap-identity)): the nesting season is over, so the pattern has ended.
- [A pattern as paw-prints](/usually-vs-must#generalization) ([a guess as fact](#trap-certainty)): a general claim rests on a trail of cases, and the ending says how long the trail is. *Always* from a few cases is a short trail, not a law.
- [*As if* as theater](/notional#notional) ([a guess as fact](#trap-certainty)): pretending is framed as a stage scene, and what really happened stays offstage.
- [Mechanism as gears](/cause#cause) ([a wish as a law](#trap-wish-as-law)): you say how a result comes about, like gears meshing, not only that it follows.
- [Feeling as a pull](/how-you-feel#urges) ([a feeling as a label](#trap-feeling)): the urge is the vowel of a little place word (*in*, *at*, *for*, *from*), so a feeling moves you in a direction, and you can notice the pull before you follow it.

Each picture already carries a warning against its trap. The intent is that it helps you reach for the healthier frame in the moment and remember it later.

## Limits {#limits}

Ageben encodes psychological concepts and techniques, but it is **not therapy, counseling, or medical advice**, and it is not a substitute for professional care when you need it.

The design aims at compassion, rationality, and empowerment, and each feature is chosen to target a common, everyday problem, drawing on research where it exists. That is design intent, not a claim that speaking or studying Ageben has been shown to improve mental health or other outcomes.

The language is currently in beta: grammar, lexicon, and these docs can change as the design settles.

## Criticisms {#criticisms}

### Can a language change how you think? {#can-a-language-change-how-you-think}

Some people doubt that a language can influence how its speakers think. The claim that it can is the weak form of the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis, and it is disputed.

I make no claim here about other languages. For Ageben specifically, I offer this argument:

1. The way a concept is framed (phrased) affects how people think about it. This is well established in [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Framing_effect_(psychology)) and the [social sciences generally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Framing_(social_sciences)).
2. Ageben's vocabulary and grammar build particular frames into how certain concepts are expressed, as laid out on this page and in the linked grammar docs.
3. So I expect Ageben to influence how its speakers think.

That is a reason to expect an effect, not a measurement of one. Whether the effect happens, and how large it is, has not been tested ([limits](#limits)).

### Ethical considerations {#ethical-considerations}

Some people are reminded of Newspeak from *1984*, where a totalitarian government alters the language to make the populace more compliant. A language designed to steer thought, on this view, is manipulation whatever its aims.

My view is that what makes Newspeak unethical is that it is forced on people who do not know what it is doing to them. A language like Ageben is ethical on three conditions: using it is voluntary, the speaker knows how it is meant to influence their thinking, and it does what it says. On those conditions, learning it is like enrolling in a self-help course to change your thought patterns.

Language influences thought but does not determine it. Speakers who want to think or communicate in a way the language discourages can still do so, though their sentences will likely be longer.

### Language evolution {#language-evolution}

Some expect that the change every language goes through over decades of use will eventually water Ageben down until it loses the properties that make it beneficial.

The language can and should change to keep up with the times, but I am optimistic that it will keep or even strengthen those properties. The people who choose to learn it want them, so I expect them to adopt changes in line with the language's goals.

Even if the language does lose its potency over a century, helping many people over that long a time would still be worth it.
