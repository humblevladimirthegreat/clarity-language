# Phonology and phonotactics
<a id="phonology"></a>

How to **sound out** Agebon. Each letter has one pronunciation. Syllables end with a consonant only at the end of the word, so you know when words stop.

## Beginner {#beginner}

Pronounce each letter the same way every time. A **syllable** is one beat with one vowel. Spell the beats you actually say: a consonant starts a beat, and the last consonant of a content word (a word built on a root, such as a noun or verb) is where that word ends.

Write native Agebon in **lowercase**. A capital means nothing extra: read it as the same letter in lowercase. Two vowel letters in a row are two syllables. Say each vowel as its own beat.

### Vowels

Agebon has four vowel letters. In English, a vowel letter’s sound depends on the letters around it; each Agebon vowel always has the same sound.

#### Opposite vowels {#opposite-vowels}

The four vowels form two **opposite** pairs. Within a pair, the tongue moves along one line and the lips change shape:

| Pair | Tongue | Lips |
|------|--------|------|
| `u` ↔ `a` | high ↔ low, centered | rounded ↔ relaxed |
| `o` ↔ `e` | back ↔ front, same height | rounded ↔ spread |

To find `u`, start from `a`: keep the tongue centered, close the jaw, and round the lips. To find `o`, start from `e`: keep the jaw where it is, pull the tongue back, and round the lips. Moving between the two vowels of a pair is a small step.

#### Vowel pitch {#vowel-pitch}

Each vowel also has its own **pitch**, always the same one: `u` is high, `e` mid-high, `o` mid-low, and `a` low. Vowel pitch is optional and adds no meaning. It backs up the vowel's sound, so a listener who half-hears a vowel can still tell which one it was. When a vowel's sound and its pitch disagree, trust the sound. The opposite vowels `a` and `u` are also the furthest apart in pitch. Keep each vowel's pitch when you say a word with feeling: make it louder, not higher.

Voices differ, so a vowel's pitch is not a fixed note but how far it sits above your **home note**, the note you say `a` on. Any comfortable note works. To practice, pick a pattern and a tempo below, move the home note with − and + until it sits easily low in your speaking voice, press **Play**, and say each vowel on its note as it lights up. Matching a note is a skill like any other, and it improves quickly with practice. Each vowel's sound already tells it apart, so your Agebon is clear while you learn.

With a microphone, **Find my home note** listens while you hold a long `a` on a comfortable low note and makes that your home note. **Check me** leaves a pause after each note for you to say it back, then marks whether you matched it to within about a semitone, came close, or were a bit high or low. Choose **From memory** to skip the notes and say each vowel unprompted. Click any note to check just that one. All listening happens in your browser: nothing you say is recorded or sent anywhere, and your home note is saved only on this device.

### Word edges

A content word ends in one of four consonants: `-l`, `-m`, `-n`, or `-r`. That last consonant is the audible end of the word. Inside the word, consonants start syllables. They do not close a syllable in the middle.

`uzozen` = *u-zo-zen*. `udozol` = *u-do-zol*.

### Phonotactics (word shape)
<a id="phonotactics"></a>

Beginner already used word edges: a content word ends in `-l` / `-m` / `-n` / `-r`. Here is the full shape of that word, in order, written as one lowercase token (`zuzozen`).

<!-- cheat-sheet: sounds-spelling -->
| Agebon | Use | English |
|--------|-----|---------|
| Role letter | first letter | role in the clause (subject, verb, …) |
| Optional `l` after `/ɡ/` | adjective before the noun | `gl-` looks ahead to the next noun |
| Optional `l` after `/b/` | [hosted extra noun](clause.md#extra-nouns) | `bl-` completes the word right before it |
| Root(s) **V(CV)+** | content | start with a vowel; each later consonant starts a new syllable |
| Mid-word `x` | productive compound seam | joins two roots inside one word |
| Mid-word `th` | stance seam | joins a sake to its stance vowel ([sakes](sakes.md)) |
| Dictionary stem (no `x`) | lexical compound | one long simple-looking root (`obahoha` *bedroom*) |
| `-l` / `-m` / `-n` / `-r` | [word ending](word-endings.md) | audible end of the content word |
| Optional `-x` | [plural](plurality.md) after the ending | word-final `-lx` / `-mx` / `-nx` / `-rx` / `-lnx` (letter `x`) |
| Stand-in `-rl` / `-rm` | [dependent clauses](dependents.md#dependent-clauses) | word-final `rl` / `rm` |

A syllable ends with a consonant only at the **end of the word**. Inside a root, `l` and `r` always have a vowel after them, so they start a syllable rather than sounding like a suffix (`zubulel`: prefix `z`, root `ubule`, ending `-l`). Each spelling has only one pronunciation.

Some short grammar words put two vowels side by side, a **vowel stack** (a join such as `ua`). A stack always puts its vowels in alphabetical order (`u`, `a`, `o`, `e`): `u` comes first and `e` comes last. That gives six stacks: `ua`, `uo`, `ue`, `ao`, `ae`, and `oe`. The two vowels stay two separate syllables.

Each vowel keeps its [pitch](#vowel-pitch) in a stack, so every stack is a small melody. The alphabet's vowel order drops from `u` to the lowest vowel and then climbs back, so the stacks mix falls and rises:

| Stack | Pitch | Contour |
|-------|-------|------|
| `ua` | high → low | big fall |
| `uo` | high → mid-low | medium fall |
| `ue` | high → mid-high | small fall, high |
| `ao` | low → mid-low | small rise, low |
| `ae` | low → mid-high | medium rise |
| `oe` | mid-low → mid-high | small rise, mid |

**A fall means `u`.** Every stack that starts with `u` falls, and every other stack rises. In grammar words a `u` stack is usually the *undo* or *set aside* member of its family, so you can hear which side a stack is on from its direction alone, before the second vowel is clear. The direction backs up the vowels; when the two disagree, trust the vowels.

**Related form:** word-initial `x` is the [continue](dependents.md#continue-x) prefix (discourse), not a compound seam.

### Number-word exception {#number-word-exception}

When English says *how many* or *which place*, Agebon uses a [number word](numbers.md). A number word puts `r` right after the role letter. Ordinary words never have that cluster, because their roots start with a vowel, so a role letter followed directly by `r` tells you a number is coming. The marker is `ra`, `ru`, `re`, or `ro`. To count from the end, write `#-` before the digits; it is spelled and spoken `rue`. Two vowels in a row stay two separate syllables.

> `zagozolx gragel.`
>
> z-cat-x | g-three
>
> "There are three cats."

`gragel` is `g` + `ra` + digit `ge` + `-l`.

### Syllabic `l` {#syllabic-l}

A [hosted extra noun](clause.md#extra-nouns) writes `l` after its `b`. When that word is a number (`r` next), say the `l` as a beat of its own, like the end of English *bottle* <IpaPlay file="En-us-bottle.mp3" label="bottle" />. The `r` then starts the next beat as usual.

| Agebon | IPA |
|--------|-----|
| `bral` | /bɹäl/ |
| `blral` | /bl̩.ɹäl/ |

This is the only beat with no vowel letter. Like a vowel, you can hold it. Before a vowel, `bl` is one ordinary start of a beat (`bluzozen` /blʉ.zo̞.ze̞n/).

## Advanced {#advanced}

### Place-name roots {#place-name-roots}

A country or territory name is spelled from the English sound. The root still starts and ends with a vowel. It may keep a consonant cluster from the English name (`avranza` from *France*). A name with no cluster is at least seven letters and ends in `n` plus that last vowel (`azabana` from *Japan*), so it does not look like a three- or five-letter dictionary root.

The ending is the ordinary name ending: `azabanan` is *Japan*, `azabanam` belonging, `azabanal` the flag ([countries](word-endings.md#countries-traditions)).

A person or place that is not in the lexicon can take the same spelling. The [lexicon](lexicon.md#respell) writes it for a name you type. To keep the original letters, use a [span](spans.md#loans).

<!-- cheat-sheet: sounds-spelling -->
| Agebon | Use | English |
|--------|-----|---------|
| `azabanan` | place-name root with no cluster (seven letters, ends `n` + vowel) | *Japan* |
| `avranzan` | place-name root that keeps a cluster | *France* |

### Singability constraints {#singability-constraints}

These choices about word shape make Agebon easier to sing:

| Constraint | Why it helps | How Agebon keeps it |
|------------|--------------|---------------------|
| One vowel per letter | Sliding from one vowel to another mid-note forces the mouth to change | Stacked vowels = separate syllables |
| Few consonant clusters (consonants with no vowel between) | Clusters are harder to say quickly | Ordinary shape is consonant-then-vowel at the start of a syllable; the few clusters are listed below |
| Voiced preferred | Unvoiced `b`, `d`, `g` (*p*, *t*, *k*) stop the note; singers often voice them anyway | Voiced preferred; unvoiced allowed as style only |
| No mid-word syllable-final consonant | Ending a syllable on a consonant breaks a held note | Roots are **V(CV)+**; a final consonant only at the word edge, the **`m`** before **`x`** on a hook prefix (its **-m** frame) or a numeric prefix (an approximate number), or the **`m`** or **`r`** before **`x`** on a hooked join (its hook's **-m** frame or **-r** point-back). The [syllabic `l`](#syllabic-l) of `blr` / `blw` is a held beat, not a final consonant |
| No lexical stress | Music already places emphasis | Stress always falls on the same beat; spelling does not mark it, and music may move it. A word's first syllable sits best on a strong beat |
| Vowel pitch | A melody owns the pitch | Song drops [vowel pitch](#vowel-pitch); each vowel's sound alone says which vowel it is |
| Spelling = pronunciation | You do not memorize special readings | One path from letters to sound |
| Audible word edges | Song often removes speech pauses | Content words end in `-l` / `-m` / `-n` / `-ln` / `-r` / `-rl` / `-rm` (optional `-x`); stand-ins end in `-rl` / `-rm` / `-rth` |

The clusters Agebon allows: left-hanging `gl-`; hosted `bl-`, with a [syllabic `l`](#syllabic-l) before `r` / `w`; number-word role letter + `r`; role letter + `w` on a [tag pronoun](pronouns.md#tag-pronouns), a [role compound](roles.md#role-compounds), a [role pointer](pronouns.md#role-pointers), or a say word (`zw`, `dw`, `bw`, `gw`, `hw`, `yw`, and `blw` on a hosted role word); mid-word `mx` after the hook vowels of a hook prefix's **-m** frame, or after the number body of an approximate [numeric prefix](numeric-derivation.md); mid-word `mx` or `rx` after the hook vowels of a hooked join (its hook's frame or point-back); word-final `-lx` / `-mx` / `-nx` / `-rx`; name instance `-ln` / `-lnx`; stand-in and sense-pinned resume `-rl` / `-rm`, stand-in `-rth`; consonant runs inside a [place-name root](#place-name-roots). The `m` or `r` before `x` is the only syllable-final consonant inside an ordinary word.

Try singing a short Agebon line quickly at a high but comfortable pitch:

`zuzozen gamahum.`

/ zʉ.zo̞.ze̞n ɡä.mä.ɦʉm /

Compare a line, not Agebon, that piles up vowels side by side, clusters, and consonants that stop the sound mid-word. It is only a little harder to speak, but much harder to hold on a note:

/ seiɹ ˈʈʂuɹt tis ˈheb.ɡiɹn fuofts /

### Rhythm and stress {#rhythm-and-stress}

Give every syllable about the same length, and keep its vowel full however fast you talk. English weakens unstressed vowels to *uh* (the second *a* in *banana*). Agebon never does, because many words differ by one vowel (`val`, `vol`, `vul`), and weak vowels would make them sound alike.

Each word takes a light stress on its **first syllable**. Since the rule never changes, spelling does not mark it. The ending consonant already tells a listener where a word stops; the stressed first beat tells them where the next one starts, so fast speech still splits into words. A [number word](numbers.md) stresses the last digit of each group instead.

`zuzozen gamahum.`

/ ˈzʉ.zo̞.ze̞n ˈɡä.mä.ɦʉm /

The first beat also keeps apart two lines that differ only by a word break: a word that starts mid-line takes a fresh stressed beat, where the same letters inside one word do not. A hooked join (*someone in there*, as one word) and a join followed by a hook (*some people*, then *in there*) can be spelled with the same letters, and this beat tells them apart.

Stress is **loudness** only: say the first syllable a little louder, with a crisp start. Its vowel keeps its own pitch, and the syllable keeps its one beat, so a stressed `a` is loud and still low. English raises and lengthens a stressed syllable, so practice stress as a drum accent: say the word on its vowel pitches, then say it again and tap the first beat louder.

**Hold the ending.** Let the final `-l`, `-m`, `-n` or `-r` sound for a moment instead of clipping it. It is often the only difference between two words (`vul`, `vum`, `vur`), and all four are sounds you can hold. In singing, put the cutoff on it.

#### Vowel pitch values {#vowel-pitch-values}

A vowel's pitch is an interval above your [home note](#vowel-pitch), not a fixed note, since voices differ by an octave or more:

| Vowel | Semitones above home note | Scale degree | Piano |
|-------|---------------------------|--------------|-------|
| `a` | 0 | 1 | D♯3 |
| `o` | +3 | ♭3 | F♯3 |
| `e` | +5 | 4 | G♯3 |
| `u` | +7 | 5 | A♯3 |

The piano keys are the [finger code](finger-code.md#piano)'s vowel keys, with D♯3 as the home note. Match the intervals, not the notes. The four pitches belong to one pentatonic scale, so any two sound well together, and a line said on its vowel pitches sounds like music. The opposite pair `a` and `u` is a fifth apart. `u` and `o`, the two rounded vowels and the closest pair by ear, are four semitones apart. The two-semitone steps (`o` to `e`, `e` to `u`) sit between vowels that already differ in lip or tongue position.

Pitch is heard against your own range, so it helps most inside a sentence, and inside a word with two vowels, which carries its own rise or fall: `yael` rises and `yuel` falls. A voice may drift lower over a long sentence; that carries nothing, as long as neighboring vowels keep their order. A whisper has no pitch, so the vowel's sound alone carries it. The [syllabic `l`](#syllabic-l) has a fixed pitch of +4 semitones, between `o` and `e`, and never copies a neighbor.

### Words never said quietly {#never-quiet}

English swallows small words that change the meaning: *can* and *can't* often differ by one weak sound, and *I think* is usually mumbled. In Agebon, give these words their full stress and a clear [pitch on each vowel](#vowel-pitch-values) even when the words around them are light:

| Words | Why |
|-------|-----|
| Negation: the `u` joins ([negation](joins.md#negation-u)) | Missing one flips the claim |
| [MAY](knowing.md#may) and its hold endings | Missing one makes a guess sound certain |
| [How you know](knowing.md#evidentiality) and [how strong the evidence is](knowing.md#evidence-strength) | Missing one hides where the claim came from |
| The kind of *can't* ([ability](intention.md#ability)) | Missing one turns *not right now* into *not ever* |

### Voice and certainty {#voice-and-certainty}

Only the words and the written [tone marks](speech-moves.md#tone-marks) carry meaning. How firmly you stand behind a claim is the ending on the [act word](speech-moves.md#speech-act); how sure you are is [MAY](knowing.md#may). Pitch carries two things only: each [vowel's own pitch](#vowel-pitch-values), and the `?` mark, which raises a word's whole range. An unwritten rise or fall adds nothing. A statement said with a rising voice is still a statement, and a firm `-l` claim said hesitantly is still firm.

So listen to the words, not the voice. A speaker whose voice naturally rises at the end is not hedging, and a confident voice is not evidence.

### Speech edges {#speech-edges}

Pitch belongs to the vowels, so the edges of a sentence are marked by words and by time. Almost every edge is a word the listener hears: a join, an `/x/` word, a `/y/` word. Only two edges have no word: a period, and a scope island (a chunk in braces, so a word inside applies only to that chunk). Those two are marked by silence counted in beats. Every syllable is one beat, so a listener hears the silence as rhythm:

- a **break**: the next beat comes half a beat late;
- a **rest**: one whole silent beat.

From smallest to largest:

| Edge | Voice | Taught in |
|------|-------|-----------|
| Word | Louder first syllable, ending held | [rhythm and stress](#rhythm-and-stress) |
| Item in a list, before the join word | Nothing extra | [right-close fence](joins.md#right-close) |
| Join word that ends a list | Nothing extra: the join word is heard | [right-close fence](joins.md#right-close) |
| Soft `-m` statement or answer | Nothing extra: the `-m` is heard | [periods](dependents.md#orthography-and-prosody-periods) |
| Scope island | Break before, one tight phrase, break after | [scope islands](spans.md#scope-islands) |
| `/x/` continue, stand-in hang | No rest: the sentence goes on | [periods](dependents.md#orthography-and-prosody-periods) |
| Period | Rest | [periods](dependents.md#orthography-and-prosody-periods) |
| Linker or topic word after a period | The period's rest; the `/x/` word says which | [periods](dependents.md#orthography-and-prosody-periods), [topic](pronouns.md#topic) |
| New `/y/` turn | The period's rest; the `/y/` word is heard | [periods](dependents.md#orthography-and-prosody-periods) |

A list needs no voice of its own, even though the join word comes last: a second word with the same role letter already tells a listener that a list is under way before the join arrives.

In song, a period takes a rest or a held note, and an island is phrased as its own short phrase.

### Singing on one note {#one-note}

Every distinction Agebon makes is spelled in its letters, so a line sung on a single pitch still says everything it says in speech. A melody owns the pitch, so song drops [vowel pitch](#vowel-pitch-values): sing each vowel on the melody's note. A songwriter may let a melody follow the vowel pitches, but no song has to. When a melody takes over your pitch, voice each [tone mark](speech-moves.md#tone-marks) with how you shape the note:

| Mark | In speech | In song |
|------|-----------|---------|
| `!` | Louder, stressed | Accent: a stronger attack, louder |
| `?` | Raised, tentative | A lighter attack, slightly late |
| `%` | Light, smiling | Short and detached |
| `&` | Stressed and slowed | Held for its full length |
| `;` | Soft, gentle | Smooth and connected, softer |

### Thinking sounds {#thinking-sounds}

When you need a moment in the middle of speaking, English fills the gap with *um* or *uh*. Agebon has two sounds for that gap, and each tells the listener what would help. Neither is a word, and neither is written.

| Sound | Means | The listener | Cue |
|-------|-------|--------------|-----|
| *mmm*, lips closed | I am thinking it over | waits and leaves the silence | Lips closed: not ready to speak |
| A held `a`, `e` or `o` (any of them), at its own pitch | I know what I mean but cannot find the word | may offer a word | Mouth open: the word is on its way |

Do not hold `u`: in grammar words `u` usually means *undo* or *not*, so a held `u` can sound like a denial. A thinking vowel is held well past one beat, so it does not sound like a [letter name](#letter-names), which is short and followed by a pause.

**Compare with:** a thinking sound only holds the gap. To fix a word you already said, use a hook: [`el`](hooks.md#rather) for a better wording of the same thing, [`ol`](hooks.md#instead) for the thing you meant instead.
