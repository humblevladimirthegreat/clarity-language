# Thanks and sorry {#thanks-and-sorry}

## The gap {#gap}

*Thanks* and *sorry* say that something passed between two people, but not what it did for anyone. A bare thank-you or apology can't show whether you noticed what was at stake, so thanks for a lift to the station sounds just like thanks for sitting up all night with someone in the hospital. In Ageben, a thanks or an apology names the **sake**: the need that the act met or harmed.

## The forms {#forms}
<!-- Agent summary: nine sakes (table); pick the row whose *What pays off* matches what the act did. Groups: `una` `uwa` `owe` `uro` (choose, do, learn, aim), `awe` `ewa` (other people), `ode` `oba` (body and senses). Can't or won't pick → `uda`. Thanks: `th` + sake + `tha` + ending (`thawetham`), after what they did, as its own sentence or after a comma; says your own sake was met. Sorry: `th` + sake + `thu` + ending, then `blawan` (*yours*), after what you did; set phrase `thawethum blawan`. Default ending `-m`. Omit when the English already says what the act did. -->

Every thanks or sorry word is built on one of nine sakes. To pick one, think about what the act did, and find the row whose *What pays off* line says it.

| Sake | Need | What pays off |
|------|------|---------------|
| `una` | autonomy | *I chose it.* |
| `uwa` | competence | *It worked; I can do this.* |
| `owe` | understanding | *It makes sense; I learned something.* |
| `uro` | purpose | *It matters.* |
| `awe` | relatedness | *We're connected.* |
| `ewa` | beneficence | *Someone is better off because of me.* |
| `ode` | pleasure | *It feels good.* |
| `oba` | physical (health, rest, safety) | *My body is cared for and safe.* |
| `uda` | a sake, unspecified | |

Nine is easier to hold as four groups, and the table lists them in order. The first four are about you as someone who chooses, does, learns, and aims: autonomy, competence, understanding, and purpose. The next two are about other people, because relatedness is your connection to them and beneficence is leaving someone better off. Pleasure and physical are about your body and senses, from enjoying a meal to sleeping well. The last row, `uda`, is for when you can't pick or would rather not, so `thudatham` thanks someone for doing you good without saying how.

To thank someone, start with `th`, add the sake, then `tha`, which says the sake was met, and finish with an ending. The ending is usually `-m`, so a thanks for feeling connected is `thawetham`, *that helped me feel connected*. The word is about you, because it says that your own sake was met, and saying it to the person who helped is what makes it a thanks. Put it after you say what they did, as a sentence of its own or after a comma.

To apologize, use `thu` in place of `tha`, which says a sake went unmet, and then add `blawan`, *yours*. Together they tell the other person that what you did cost them something. Learn `thawethum blawan`, *that hurt your sense of connection*, as a set phrase, and build other apologies by swapping in a different sake. Put it after you say what you did.

You don't always need the drop-in. When your English already says what the act did for you, as in *thanks for the ride, I'd have been soaked*, the listener can already hear the sake, and a plain thanks says it all.

## Whose sake it is {#whose-sake}
<!-- Agent summary: thanks names your own met sake; sorry names the listener's unmet sake via `blawan`. A lone `thu` word (`thawethum`) says your own sake went unmet: a cost to you, not an apology. Being helped never serves your `ewa`: the helper's beneficence is theirs; thank with whichever of your sakes the help met (`thobatham`, `thawetham`). -->

A thanks and a sorry look in opposite directions. A thanks talks about your sake, meaning what the other person's act did for you. A sorry talks about theirs, meaning what your act cost them.

That's why the sorry needs `blawan` and the thanks doesn't. Without it, a `thu` word is about your own sake, so it names a cost to you:

> You skipped my party. `thawethum`.  
> I skipped your party. `thawethum blawan`.

The first tells a friend that their absence hurt your sense of connection. That's a fair thing to say, but it isn't an apology. The second owns up to the harm you did to their connection with you.

The same direction can trip you up with thanks. When a friend helps you move, it's tempting to reach for beneficence, because helping is what they did. Beneficence belongs to the person whose act left someone better off, so here it's your friend's. Your thanks names what the help did for you instead: rest for your back (`thobatham`), or the sense that someone shows up for you (`thawetham`).

## Telling sakes apart {#telling-apart}
<!-- Agent summary: near misses. Competence `uwa` = it works; understanding `owe` = it makes sense (you can fix what you don't understand). Competence = how well it goes; purpose `uro` = whether it's worth doing (a failed try at something that matters still serves purpose). Relatedness `awe` = the bond, both ways; beneficence `ewa` = your act left someone better off, with or without a bond. -->

A few sakes sit close together, and the same favor can seem to fit either one until you ask what actually paid off.

### It works or it makes sense {#works-or-makes-sense}

Competence is about getting something to work, and understanding is about it making sense. You can fix a machine you don't understand, so the two often come apart:

> You walked me through the router setup. `thuwatham`.  
> You walked me through the router setup. `thowetham`.

With `thuwatham`, the wifi works and you're thanking them for that. With `thowetham`, you finally get how the router works, which is a different thing to be grateful for.

### It works or it matters {#works-or-matters}

Competence is how well something goes, and purpose is whether it's worth doing at all. Because a try at something that matters serves purpose even when it fails, the two can point at the same help in different ways:

> You read my application for the clinic job. `thuwatham`.  
> You read my application for the clinic job. `thurotham`.

With `thuwatham`, their notes made the application better. With `thurotham`, you're thanking them for helping with something that matters to you, and that holds even if you don't get the job.

### The bond or the effect {#bond-or-effect}

Relatedness lives in the bond between people, and it runs both ways. Beneficence lives in the effect, when your act leaves someone better off, with or without a bond. So when a friend brings you along to volunteer at the food bank, you can thank them for either:

> You brought me to the food bank. `thawetham`.  
> You brought me to the food bank. `thewatham`.

With `thawetham`, the afternoon you spent together is what paid off. With `thewatham`, it's the families who ate because you showed up.

## How long it lasts {#endings}
<!-- Agent summary: on thanks, ending = how long the payoff lasts: `-l` it will stay with me, `-m` (default) unstated, `-r` got me through right now. On sorry, = how lasting the harm is: `-l` fixed as far as you can tell (deeply sorry), `-m` (default) effort or circumstance may soften it, `-r` passing (my bad). Neither end is better. Unsure → `-m`. Same direction as How sure are you? (`-l` ≈ lasting, `-r` ≈ right now). -->

The last letter of a thanks or sorry word is its ending, and it says how long the effect lasts. You met these three endings in *How sure are you?*, where you could think of `-l` as *lasting* and `-r` as *right now*. They run in the same direction here, with `-m` as the everyday default between them. If you're not sure which ending fits, use `-m`.

| Ending | Thanks (how long the payoff lasts) | Sorry (how lasting the harm is) |
|--------|------------------------------------|---------------------------------|
| `-l` | it will stay with me | I'm deeply sorry |
| `-m` | thank you (the default) | I'm sorry (the default) |
| `-r` | that got me through right now | my bad |

On a thanks, the ending says how long the payoff will last:

> You helped me with the spreadsheet. `thuwathar`.  
> You helped me with the spreadsheet. `thuwathal`.

With `thuwathar`, the help got today's report out on time. With `thuwathal`, you can now build the next one yourself, and that will stay with you. Neither is the better thanks, because help that lands right when you need it is often exactly what was needed.

On a sorry, the ending says how lasting the harm is:

> I missed your recital. `thawethur blawan`.  
> I missed your recital. `thawethul blawan`.

With `thawethur blawan`, there's another recital next month, and the harm will pass. With `thawethul blawan`, it was the last one, and as far as you can tell nothing will make up for it. The default `-m` sits between them: the harm is real, and effort or a change in circumstance could soften it.

## Thanks for something you own {#possession}
<!-- Agent summary: thank a thing you own: `g` for `th`, right after the English noun (*my coat* `gobathal`); the word already says it's yours. Only for things you own; for anything else, thank the event with the `th` word after the clause. -->

You can also thank a thing, such as a coat that has kept you warm for years. Start the word with `g` instead of `th`, and put it right after the English noun. *My old coat `gobathal`* says the coat itself keeps your body well, and its `-l` says that care is lasting. The `g` word also says the coat is yours.

Because of that, use it only for things you own. When the thing belongs to someone else, thank the event instead, with the `th` word after the clause:

> My boots `gobatham` kept me dry.  
> Your boots kept me dry. `thobatham`.

In the first, the boots are yours, and you're thanking them directly. In the second, they're your friend's, and putting `gobatham` after *your boots* would claim them as yours, so the thanks goes to what happened: their boots keeping you dry.

## Examples {#examples}

> You covered my shift. `thobatham`.  
> I cancelled again. `thawethum blawan`.  
> Thanks for teaching me the shortcut, `thuwathal`.  
> You let me plan the trip my way. `thunatham`.  
> My old coat `gobathal` has lasted ten winters.  
> I spoiled the ending. `thodethur blawan`.

`thobatham` thanks someone for the rest they gave you. *Thanks for teaching me the shortcut* keeps the English thanks and adds what it did: `thuwathal` says the skill will keep paying off. `thodethur blawan` owns a small, passing harm to someone's enjoyment of the story.
