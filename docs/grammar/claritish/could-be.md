# How sure are you? {#could-be}

## The gap {#gap}

English *I think* often hides a guess, and a painful guess said that way is easy to start treating as a fact. *Probably*, *maybe*, and *definitely* are vague words, and two people can read the same one very differently. Ageben has one word for *could be*, and its ending says what you plan to do about your guess.

## The forms {#forms}
<!-- Agent summary: three drop-ins for could be (table): says it could be so, not that it is, and gives no reason. Ending = what you'll do about the guess: `thomal` find out, `thomam` (default) unsaid, `thomar` let it pass. Placement: where English puts *probably*; covers its clause. Unsure → `thomam`. Omit when you mean it is so. Most useful on painful guesses. -->

Each drop-in says that something *could be* so, without claiming that it is. It gives no reason for thinking so, either. It just marks the sentence as a guess, so the listener doesn't take it as news.

The three differ only in their last letter. To choose, ask yourself what you're going to do about the guess: find out, say nothing about it either way, or let it go.

| Drop-in | Meaning | What you'll do about the guess |
|---------|---------|--------------------------------|
| `thomal` | could be, and I'll find out | settle it |
| `thomam` | could be (the default) | leave that unsaid |
| `thomar` | could be, who knows | let it pass |

Put the drop-in where English would put *probably*: at the end of the clause, at the start, or next to the word it colors. It covers the clause it sits in. When you can't tell which ending fits, use `thomam`, because it says *could be* and nothing about what comes next.

The word earns its place on painful guesses, such as a rejection, a loss, or a friend who seems upset with you. Those are the guesses that *I think* most easily turns into facts. When you mean that something is so, leave the drop-in out, and the plain sentence says it.

## What the ending says {#endings}
<!-- Agent summary: ending = follow-up on the guess: `-l` you will find out, `-m` (default) unsaid, `-r` a passing thought you're not pursuing right now. None is better. This is the settled-to-passing scale: `-l` most settled (≈ lasting), `-m` default, `-r` most passing (≈ right now). It recurs on other drop-ins; each word gives the three endings its own meaning, same direction. -->

The last letter of the drop-in is its ending, and here it says whether you'll follow up on the guess. Take one worry, said three ways:

> She's upset with me `thomal`.  
> She's upset with me `thomam`.  
> She's upset with me `thomar`.

With `thomal`, you're going to find out, perhaps by asking her tonight. With `thomam`, you're saying it could be and leaving the next step unsaid. With `thomar`, it's a passing thought: it crossed your mind, and you aren't chasing it right now.

None of the three is the right one. Checking, leaving a guess at *could be*, and letting it go are all good choices, so pick the one that matches what you'll actually do.

You'll meet these three endings, `-l`, `-m`, and `-r`, again and again on other drop-ins. Together they make the **settled-to-passing scale**, which runs from the most settled end to the most passing one. `-l` is the settled end, and here it means the question will get settled. `-m` sits in the middle as the default. `-r` is the passing end, and here it means a thought that is only for right now.

Each drop-in has its own meaning for the three endings, but the direction never changes, so it pays to fix it in memory now. Think of `-l` as *lasting* and `-r` as *right now*. When a later drop-in takes an ending, start from that picture and ask what lasting and right now mean for that word.

## Examples {#examples}

> The package arrives Friday `thomam`.  
> My application got rejected `thomam`.  
> It'll rain `thomar`.  
> I left the stove on `thomal`.  
> The flight's delayed `thomam`.

*I left the stove on `thomal`* says you might have, and you're going back to check. *It'll rain `thomar`* is a thought you're not chasing: it crossed your mind, and you won't be checking the forecast. *My application got rejected `thomam`* keeps a painful guess where it belongs: it could be so, and the sentence doesn't pretend you've heard back.

## Telling a guess apart {#telling-apart}
<!-- Agent summary: near misses. No drop-in = saying it is so; a later plain sentence settles the guess and doesn't contradict it. *I think* reporting the act of thinking (*I'm thinking about the trip*) is no guess and stays plain English. Claims only: on a command or request the drop-in has no reading. -->

A guess is easy to confuse with three other things, and each of them is handled differently.

### A guess or a fact {#guess-or-fact}

Without the drop-in, a sentence says that something is so. *The flight's delayed* passes on news, and *the flight's delayed `thomam`* only says it could be. So when you've seen the departures board yourself, leave the drop-in out.

A plain sentence later on doesn't take the guess back. Suppose you said *I left the stove on `thomal`* and then found the stove off. Saying *the stove is off* settles the question, and you weren't wrong earlier, because you never claimed the stove was on.

### A guess or a thought {#guess-or-thought}

When *I think* reports what's going on in your head, as in *I'm thinking about the trip*, it isn't a guess, and it stays in plain English. The drop-in is for the other *I think*, the one in *I think she's upset with me*. There you could swap in *could be* without changing what you mean, and that swap is a good test.

### A claim or a request {#claim-or-request}

The drop-in goes on a claim, a sentence that says how things are or could be. A request or an order isn't a claim, so there's nothing in it for the drop-in to mark. For example, *close the window `thomam`* isn't a softer or more tentative request. It has no meaning at all, so leave the drop-in off and ask in plain English.

## Saying the words {#saying}
<!-- Agent summary: pronunciation keywords (table). Each letter one sound, always the same; each vowel its own beat (`thomam` = two beats); two vowel letters = two beats. Light stress on the first beat; vowels stay full, never *uh*. -->

Every drop-in in this course is a real Ageben word, so it's worth saying it the Ageben way from the start. Each letter has one sound, the same every time, and every vowel gets its own beat. So `thomam` has two beats, one for each vowel. Two vowel letters in a row make two beats as well.

| Letter | Sounds like |
|--------|-------------|
| `a` | *spa* |
| `e` | *bet* |
| `o` | *no*, without the glide |
| `u` | *you*, without the *y* |
| `th` | the sound in *this* |

Give the first beat a light stress, just a little louder than the rest. Keep the other vowels full, too. English shrinks unstressed vowels to *uh*, like the second *a* in *banana*, but an Ageben vowel keeps its sound however fast you talk.
