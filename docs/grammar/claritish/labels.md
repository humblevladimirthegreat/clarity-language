# Labels {#labels}

## The gap {#gap}

*He's a liar* turns one act into someone's nature. English needs a whole extra clause to take a label back (*well, he lied this one time*). Ageben puts the **label reach**, how far a label applies, inside the word itself.

## The forms {#forms}
<!-- Agent summary: label reach = English word + hyphen + `th` + reach vowel (table): `-thu` this episode, `-the` one relationship, `-tho` a practiced role, `-tha` a type across scenes; vowels small → big: `u`, `e`, `o`, `a`. To mark the label reach, add a hyphen to the English word, then `th` and the **reach vowel**, which says how far the label reaches:

| Suffix | Reach | English often says |
|--------|-------|--------------------|
| `-thu` | this episode: this stretch of doing | *this time*, *just this once* |
| `-the` | this pair: true in one relationship | *with him*, *to her at least* |
| `-tho` | a practiced role: the office they inhabit | *as her job*, *a nurse* |
| `-tha` | a type across scenes | *an anxious type*, *that kind of person* |

The rows run from the smallest reach to the biggest, so the vowel tells you the size of the claim. With `u`, the label covers a speck of time, one stretch of doing. With `e`, it covers one person: how someone is toward that one other person. With `o`, it covers a life they inhabit, such as a job or a part they play, and with `a` it covers their scenes in general, because the quality follows them around.

The suffix goes straight onto the word that carries the label. For an act, that's the verb, as in *he `lied-thu`*. For a quality it's the adjective (*I was `lazy-thu`*), and for a role it's the noun (*she's a `nurse-tho`*). The hyphen only shows that the word in front is English. The letters after it are spelled exactly as in Ageben.

You met the endings `-l`, `-m`, and `-r` in *How sure are you?*, where they say how settled something is, with `-l` as *lasting* and `-r` as *right now*. They don't come back here. A reach suffix stops at its vowel, because the vowel is the whole choice and there's nothing left for an ending to say.

You don't need a suffix on every label. When the reach doesn't matter, as in *I'm tired* after a long flight, leave it off, since a bare word makes no claim about how far it reaches. Add the suffix when you'd otherwise reach for *this time* or *a teacher*, or when a listener could hear one act as a whole nature.

When you can't tell which reach fits, go by what you've actually seen. If all you've seen is one episode, `-thu` says exactly that much and no more.

## One label, four reaches {#four-reaches}
<!-- Agent summary: any label takes any reach vowel; the English word stays, the claim's size changes. `strict-thu` one occasion; `strict-the` toward one person (name them in English after it, or leave them unstated); `strict-tho` the role they practice, which stays in its scenes; `strict-tha` a type, the quality follows them. On a verb, `-tho` = the act as their practice (`lies-tho`), vs one act `lied-thu`. -->

Any label can take any of the four vowels, and the English word stays the same. What changes is how much of the person you're making a claim about. Picture a soccer coach and four people who each want to say *she's strict*:

> She's `strict-thu`.  
> She's `strict-the` with her son.  
> She's `strict-tho`.  
> She's `strict-tha`.

The first is about one occasion. Today at practice she made the whole team run laps for being late, and the sentence says nothing about tomorrow. The second keeps the label inside one relationship: with her son she's strict, and with everyone else she might be easygoing. You name the other person in English, the way you normally would. If you leave them out (*she's `strict-the`*), the label still holds toward one person, but you don't say who.

The third is about the role she practices. Strictness is part of how she coaches, the office she steps into at the field, and at home it may not show at all. The fourth calls her a strict type, someone the quality follows from scene to scene: at the field, with friends, and at dinner.

A verb works the same way. *He `lied-thu`* is one lie. *He `lies-tho`* makes lying his practice, the way it is for a con artist, so it says much more than one outing.

## Telling reaches apart {#telling-apart}
<!-- Agent summary: near misses. `-thu` = one occasion vs `-the` = one relationship, both may take *to him*. `-tho` = a role, kept to the scenes where it's practiced, vs `-tha` = a type, across scenes. *always* / *often* / *only at work* stay English and say on which occasions; the reach vowel says what kind of claim; `-tha` allows exceptions, *always* claims every time; *often* is not `-tho`; *always* + `-tha` doubles down. -->

A few reaches get confused with each other, usually because the English around them sounds alike.

### One time or one person {#time-or-person}

Both reaches are small, and both can come with *to him*. *She was `rude-thu` to him* is about one occasion, such as the meeting where she snapped at him. *She's `rude-the` to him* is about the relationship. It's how she is with him, meeting after meeting, while with other people she's polite. So ask whether the label stays in one moment or follows one pair of people.

### A role or a type {#role-or-type}

A role stays in the scenes where someone plays it. A salesman who is `pushy-tho` presses hard on the sales floor, because pressing is part of the job he practices, and he may be mild at home. A salesman who is `pushy-tha` is a pushy type. He presses on the sales floor, at dinner, and in line at the bank.

### A type or every time {#type-or-always}

English words like *always*, *often*, and *only at work* say on which occasions something holds, and they stay in English. The reach vowel says what kind of claim the label is. For example, *he's always late* claims every single time, while *he's `late-tha`* treats him as a late type and still leaves room for the morning he showed up early.

In the same way, *often* is a count of occasions and isn't what `-tho` means. *He often lies* says it happens a lot, and *he `lies-tho`* names a practice. You can also use both kinds of word at once: *he's always `late-tha`* doubles down.

## Examples {#examples}

> He `lied-thu`. He's not a `liar-tho`.  
> I was `lazy-thu` this morning.  
> She's `rude-the`, to him at least.  
> We were `careless-thu` on that release.  
> He's `anxious-tha`.

The first line says one act happened, and denies that lying is his practice. *She's `rude-the`, to him at least* keeps the label inside one relationship, so it says nothing about how she treats anyone else. *He's `anxious-tha`* does call him a type. The suffix makes that a choice the listener can see, instead of something a bare *he's anxious* slips in.
