# Allowed and required {#allowed-required-agreed}

## The gap {#gap}

English *can*, *may*, *should*, and *OK* blur who allowed something, and whether the person it affects agreed or was only assumed to. When nobody says whose yes it is, a habit everyone follows can pass for a rule, and a guess that a friend won't mind can pass for their actual yes. Ageben has separate words for **permission** (someone allowing or forbidding an act), **requirement** (someone demanding it), and **consent** (the yes of the person the act affects), and the ending of each word says where the yes or no comes from.

## The forms {#forms}
<!-- Agent summary: four drop-ins (table), ending `-m` by default: `hwuvam` allowed, `hwuvum` forbidden, `hwuvom` required (someone over the act); `hwudam` agreed (consent of the person it's done to). Placement: after the clause, before it, or beside the word it's about; covers the whole clause. `-m` with no one named: the person with the say is the speaker. Unsure of the source → `-m`. Each drop-in below says what someone said about an act. The first three come from someone outside the act, such as a rule or a person with the standing to decide, and they say it's allowed, forbidden, or required. The fourth is consent, which comes from inside the act. It says that the person the act is done to said yes, because it's their body, their things, or their time that the act touches.

| Drop-in | What was said | English often says |
|---------|---------------|--------------------|
| `hwuvam` | someone allows the act | *you may*, *I'm letting you* |
| `hwuvum` | someone forbids the act | *you may not* |
| `hwuvom` | someone requires the act | *you have to* |
| `hwudam` | the person it's done to agreed | *they agreed* |

The drop-in goes where English would put an adverb: after the clause, before it, or right beside the word it's about. Wherever it sits, it covers the whole clause, so *you can leave early `hwuvam`* gives permission for the leaving, not just for the word next to it.

All four are shown with `-m`, the default ending, which means a person said so. When nobody else is named, that person is you, the speaker. So *you can leave early `hwuvam`* means you're the one letting them go, and *no phones at dinner `hwuvum`* means you're the one saying no. The next section shows how the ending changes to name a rule or no one at all, and if you can't tell where the yes or no comes from, keep `-m`.

You don't need a drop-in when the English already says whose say it is. *The rules say we can park here* and *she said it's fine* have done the job themselves, so leave them as they are. The drop-in earns its place when the source is easy to lose: a bare *you can*, *you have to*, or *it's OK* that someone may later act on.

## Where the yes or no comes from {#source}
<!-- Agent summary: ending grades the source, not the settled-to-passing scale: `-l` a rule or binding agreement (≈ law), `-m` (default) a person said so (≈ mouth), `-r` nobody said: tolerated, frowned on, customary, or assumed (≈ read the room). All twelve: table (rows = what was said, columns = source). Consent endings = how binding the yes is: `hwudal` agreed in advance, binding; `hwudam` a yes they can take back; `hwudar` nobody asked, taken as given (allowed; says *I assumed* openly). Different sources never clash: `hwuval` beside `hwuvur`. -->

You met three endings in *How sure are you?*, where they ran from settled to passing: think of `-l` as *lasting* and `-r` as *right now*. These drop-ins use the same three letters for something else. They don't say how settled anything is. They say where the yes or no comes from.

`-l` is a rule (think *law*). `-m` is a person's word, and it's the default (think *mouth*). `-r` is no one's word at all, only what people put up with, frown on, or expect without anyone saying so (think *read the room*). Take one requirement, said three ways:

> Guests take their shoes off `hwuvol`.  
> Guests take their shoes off `hwuvom`.  
> Guests take their shoes off `hwuvor`.

With `hwuvol`, a rule requires it, such as a sign by the door or the building's policy. With `hwuvom`, you're the one asking them to, perhaps because it's your home. With `hwuvor`, nobody has asked anyone: it's simply what guests here do.

Each row of the table below is one thing that can be said about an act, and each column is one source. So once you know the four rows and the three sources, you can find all twelve words, and every word in a column ends in the same letter.

| What was said | `-l` a rule | `-m` a person's word | `-r` no one's word |
|---------------|-------------|----------------------|--------------------|
| allowed | `hwuval` *the rules allow it* | `hwuvam` *I'm letting you* | `hwuvar` *no one's stopping it* |
| forbidden | `hwuvul` *the rules forbid it* | `hwuvum` *you may not* | `hwuvur` *people won't like it* |
| required | `hwuvol` *the rules require it* | `hwuvom` *you have to* | `hwuvor` *it's customary* |
| agreed | `hwudal` *they signed on in advance* | `hwudam` *they said yes* | `hwudar` *they're probably OK with it* |

Because each ending names a different source, two of them can be true of the same act without contradicting each other. Jeans at the office can be allowed by the rules (`hwuval`) and still frowned on (`hwuvur`), so a new hire who asks whether jeans are OK may need both answers.

### How binding a yes is {#binding}

On the consent word, the ending says how firmly the person agreed. Take one photo, said three ways:

> The newsletter printed her photo `hwudal`.  
> The newsletter printed her photo `hwudam`.  
> The newsletter printed her photo `hwudar`.

With `hwudal`, she signed on in advance, perhaps with a release form, and backing out now has terms. With `hwudam`, she said yes, and she can take it back at any time. With `hwudar`, nobody asked her, and the newsletter took her yes as given.

`hwudar` is a fair thing to say, but it's visibly weaker than the other two. It puts *I assumed* into the sentence, where English would hide it inside *she was fine with it*.

## Telling them apart {#telling-apart}
<!-- Agent summary: near misses. Rule (`-l`) vs custom (`-r`): `hwuvol` / `hwuvor`, `hwuvul` / `hwuvur`. Permission (`hwuvam`, someone over the act) vs consent (`hwudam`, the person it's done to); both can hold. `-m` on `hwuvam` / `hwuvum` / `hwuvom` = the speaker's say, so no reading on a sentence about the speaker (*I have to …*): use `hwuvol`, `hwuvor`, or name the person in English and leave the drop-in out. *can* = able (*I can swim*) involves no one's yes → no drop-in. -->

A few pairs get confused, usually because English uses the same words for both.

### A rule or a custom {#rule-or-custom}

*Have to* sounds the same whether a rule demands something or everyone just does it. *Phones go off during the meeting `hwuvol`* points to a rule someone could show you. *Phones go off during the meeting `hwuvor`* says nobody ever made it a rule, though everyone in the room expects it.

The same split works for a ban. *No smoking on the balcony `hwuvul`* means the rules forbid it, while *no smoking on the balcony `hwuvur`* means you could, but the neighbors won't like it. A newcomer who hears a custom as a rule may worry about breaking something that was never written down.

### Someone allowing it or the person agreeing {#permission-or-consent}

Permission comes from someone over the act, while consent comes from the person the act is done to, and the two can come apart. *The doctor examined my son `hwuvam`* says you, the parent, allowed it. *The doctor examined my son `hwudam`* says your son agreed himself. Both can be true, and saying which one you mean keeps the parent's yes from passing for the child's.

### A sentence about yourself {#about-yourself}

Because `-m` with nobody named means the say is yours, `hwuvam`, `hwuvum`, and `hwuvom` don't fit a sentence about what you yourself do. *I have to work late `hwuvom`* would make you the outside person demanding it of yourself, and that has no reading. When something is required of you, name where the demand comes from instead. Use `hwuvol` when a rule requires it and `hwuvor` when it's custom, or name the person in English (*my manager says I have to work late*) and leave the drop-in out.

### Allowed or able {#allowed-or-able}

In *Can and can't*, the *can* that means someone is able to do something got its own suffix on the verb. That *can* involves no one's yes. *I can swim* is about a skill, not a permission, so none of these drop-ins fits it. They're for the other *can*, the one that means someone allows it, as in *you can park here* when the rules allow it (`hwuval`).

## Examples {#examples}

> I borrowed your car `hwudar`.  
> You can leave early `hwuvam`.  
> We wear ties `hwuvor`, not `hwuvol`.  
> Visitors sign in at the desk `hwuvol`.  
> No phones at dinner `hwuvum`.  
> People smoke on the balcony `hwuvar`.

*I borrowed your car `hwudar`* owns up to the assumption: you didn't ask, and you're saying so. *We wear ties `hwuvor`, not `hwuvol`* tells a new colleague that ties are a custom, so nobody will point to a rule if they skip one. *People smoke on the balcony `hwuvar`* doesn't say anyone approved, only that nobody is stopping them.
