# Proposal: Claritish (Agazan drop-ins for English)

**Status:** PROPOSED  

**Design authority:** remains [`docs/grammar/`](../grammar/introduction.md). Claritish adds no forms. Each drop-in is an existing Agazan word, spelled exactly as the grammar page that teaches it spells it.

## Motivation

The full language takes a long time before it pays off. Most of Agazan's psychological value sits in a few closed words: stance words, endings and tone marks. English has no word for these, yet each one makes sense by itself. If English speakers can drop those words into ordinary English sentences, they get the benefit on day one, and each word they learn is already real Agazan.

## Goals

1. Speakers get **immediate benefit**: calibrated *could be*, honest sourcing, less labeling of people, thanks and apologies that name the need, clear consent, decisions that say how final they are, and feelings named without a judgment.
2. **Nothing is wasted.** Every form is spelled exactly as in Agazan, so learning Claritish is learning Agazan.
3. **Excitement.** Each lesson ends with a taste of the same idea in a full Agazan sentence. The last page invites the learner to start the full language.
4. **No new grammar.** A drop-in sits in an English sentence without any Agazan syntax around it.

## Non-goals

- Agazan syntax in English (role letters, hosted `/b/`, joins, spans, hooks, role compounds, numeric derivation).
- Respelling for English readers, or marking Agazan words with italics, quotes or brackets. Drop-ins are written plainly.
- Numerals, stance numbers, or other number-system forms. Those wait for the full language.
- A replacement for the grammar track. Claritish is an on-ramp.

## Rules

1. **A drop-in is a closed Agazan word**, spelled exactly as in Agazan: a tone mark, a stance word, a sake word (thanks, sorry, feelings), or the set phrase `behodon`.
2. **Placement is free and follows English.** Put the word where an English adverb or tag would sit: after the clause (`She'll come thovum.`), before it, or next to the word it colors.
3. **Scope is the nearest clause.** A stance drop-in covers the English clause it sits in.
4. **The one suffix on English words is written with a hyphen.** It is [label scope](../grammar/predication.md#label-scope): English word + hyphen + `th` + scope vowel + **-l** (`lied-thal`). The letters after the hyphen are the Agazan spelling; the hyphen only marks that the host is English, not an Agazan root. Always **-l**. That ending is not “concrete vs abstract”; Claritish does not teach that split.
5. **Tone marks** go before what they color, as in Agazan: `%` before a word, or a free-standing `%` before the rest of the sentence. Agazan `!` / `!!` also go *before* what they color. A sentence-final English `!` is still English punctuation.
6. **English punctuation stays English.** Sentences don't have to end in an Agazan period.

**-l / -m / -r** on stance drop-ins grade the stance on the **settled-to-passing** scale in most lessons: **-l** is the most settled, **-m** is the default, and **-r** the most passing. Lessons teach the cue *-l ≈ lasting, -r ≈ right now* and point out each time the scale comes back. Lesson 6 grades the source instead (*l ≈ law, m ≈ mouth, r ≈ read the room*). These endings are never the citation split between a concrete picture and a published abstract.

## Where it lives

Claritish is its own site section, outside the stage order, like the *Saying it in Agazan* recipe track. [Why Agazan](../grammar/why-agazan.md) and [introduction](../grammar/introduction.md) link to Lesson 1 as the easy entry point. Stage pages do not link to it.

## Lessons

Follow the lessons in order. Each one is a single page with: the gap in English, the forms (a small table), five or so English example sentences, a short practice, and a **Full Agazan** box that shows one Agazan sentence using the form, as a teaser. This proposal gives only the forms and the English examples. Practice and the Full Agazan box are written when the lessons are implemented. Examples are always English sentences with drop-ins, never full Agazan.

**No links into the grammar pages.** The grammar has to be learned in order, so lesson pages never link to a stage page, not even from the Full Agazan box. The only way from Claritish into the grammar is the closing plug after Lesson 8. The **Source** lines below are for editors only and don't appear on the lesson pages. The order goes from zero-cost marks, through the rationality and compassion words, and finishes on the richest compose.

### Lesson 1: Tone marks

**Gap:** tone is lost in text. Is it a joke, sincere, or said with emphasis?

| Form | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| `%` | joking, teasing |
| `;` | warm, affectionate |
| `&` | contrast: this one, not another |
| `?` | unsure about this word |
| `?!` | surprised and doubtful |
| `!` | strong feeling (the words say which) |
| `!!` | stronger still |

> %You're the worst.  
> ;Call me when you land.  
> We went &Tuesday.  
> He brought a ?casserole.  
> !We shipped it.

**Why first:** there's nothing to pronounce, and people can use it in their very next text. It also teaches the Agazan rule that a mark goes *before* what it colors. **Source** (editors): [tone marks](../grammar/speech-moves.md#tone-marks).

### Lesson 2: How sure are you?

**Gap:** English *I think* often smuggles a guess. *Probably* / *maybe* / *definitely* are vague, and people read them very differently.

| Form | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| `thovum` | could be (default) |
| `thovul` | could be, and I'll find out |
| `thovur` | could be, who knows (a passing thought) |

> The package arrives Friday thovum.  
> She's upset with me thovul.  
> It'll rain thovur.  
> I left the stove on thovul.  
> The flight's delayed thovum.

**Why here:** it's the clearest rationality win, and it introduces the **-l / -m / -r** endings on the **settled-to-passing** scale: **-l** the most settled (you'll settle it), **-m** the default, **-r** the most passing (a passing thought, right now only). Teach the cue *-l ≈ lasting, -r ≈ right now* here. **Source** (editors): [MAY](../grammar/knowing.md#may).

### Lesson 3: How do you know?

**Gap:** English doesn't mark whether a claim was seen, remembered, inferred or heard. Rumors take on the authority of eyewitness reports.

| Form | Channel |
|------|---------|
| `thodom` | live / watching it now |
| `thevom` | from memory (a past observation) |
| `therem` | on record / playback / scheduled |
| `thobam` | from the pattern |
| `thunem` | inferred from clues |
| `thewam` | someone told me |
| `thahom` | gut feeling |
| `thozem` | per the tale |

The ending swap is the payoff: **-l** strong evidence, **-m** default, **-r** weak (`thewar` a rumor, `thevol` a vivid memory, `thahor` a faint hunch). Evidence strength is how much the claim rests on, not how likely the event is.

> The server is down thodom.  
> Marco quit thewar.  
> The meeting got moved therel.  
> I locked the door thevor.  
> This bug is in the parser thunem, thovum.

The last example shows that how you know and *could be* are separate. **Why here:** the endings from Lesson 2 come back on the same settled-to-passing scale (solid evidence is lasting; a rumor could shift any moment), so the system starts to feel learnable. **Source** (editors): [evidentiality](../grammar/knowing.md#evidentiality), [evidence strength](../grammar/knowing.md#evidence-strength).

### Lesson 4: Labels (this time, a role, to whom, a type)

**Gap:** *He's a liar* turns one act into someone's nature. English needs a whole clause to take that back.

Add a hyphen, then `th` + a scope vowel + **-l**, to the English word:

| Suffix | Reach |
|--------|-------|
| `…-thal` | this episode: this stretch of doing |
| `…-thel` | a practiced role: the office they inhabit |
| `…-thol` | this pair: true in one relationship |
| `…-thul` | a type across scenes |

> He lied-thal. He's not a liar-thel.  
> I was lazy-thal this morning.  
> She's rude-thol, to him at least.  
> We were careless-thal on that release.  
> He's anxious-thul.

**Why here:** this is the flagship compassion feature, and it's the most striking thing to show people. The letters after `th` are the Agazan seam exactly, so `valahathal` looks familiar right away. **Source** (editors): [label scope](../grammar/predication.md#label-scope).

### Lesson 5: Thanks and sorry that say why

**Gap:** *thanks* and *sorry* don't say what the act did for the person.

| Form | Sake |
|------|------|
| `ahu` | autonomy |
| `ulo` | competence |
| `ado` | understanding |
| `ame` | purpose |
| `ana` | relatedness |
| `ebe` | beneficence |
| `ozo` | pleasure |
| `oyu` | physical (health, rest, safety) |
| `ega` | a sake, unspecified |

Thanks = sake + `tha` + ending (met): `thanatham` *that helped me feel connected*, `thulothal` *that sets me up for good*. Sorry = sake + `thu` + ending, then `behodon` (unmet, *yours*): `thanathum behodon` *that hurt your sense of connection*. **-l / -m / -r** are the settled-to-passing scale again: how long the payoff lasts (thanks) or how lasting the harm is (sorry), from lasting (**-l**) to only right now (**-r**, `…thur behodon` *my bad*).

> You covered my shift — thoyutham.  
> I cancelled again. Thanathum behodon.  
> Thanks for teaching me the shortcut, thulothal.  
> You listened all evening — thadotham.  
> I spoiled the ending. Thozothur behodon.

**Why here:** the sakes are the heart of the psychology, and naming a need is a skill people can use every day. `behodon` is the first Agazan word outside the stance set; teach it as a set phrase. **Source** (editors): [thanks and sorry](../grammar/sakes.md#thanks-sorry), [sake inventory](../grammar/sakes.md#sake-inventory).

### Lesson 6: Allowed, required, agreed

**Gap:** English *can* / *may* / *should* / *OK* blur who allowed something, and whether consent was given or assumed.

| Form | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| `thegol` / `thegom` / `thegor` | allowed by rule / by a person / tolerated |
| `thedel` / `thedem` / `theder` | forbidden by rule / by a person / frowned on |
| `thumel` / `thumem` / `thumer` | required by rule / by a person / by custom |
| `thuxegol` / `thuxegom` / `thuxegor` | the affected person agreed in advance / said yes (revocable) / is assumed OK |

> I borrowed your car thuxegor — I should have asked for thuxegom.  
> You can leave early thegom.  
> We wear ties thumer, not thumel.  
> Visitors sign in at the desk thumel.  
> No phones at dinner thedem.

**Why here:** the **-l / -m / -r** endings come back with a new job: where the yes or no comes from. Teach the cue *l ≈ law, m ≈ mouth, r ≈ read the room*: a rule, a person's word (the default), or no one's word at all. **Sources** (editors): [permission](../grammar/sakes.md#permission), [requirement](../grammar/sakes.md#requirement), [consent](../grammar/sakes.md#consent).

### Lesson 7: Decisions and tries

**Gap:** *I've decided* sounds final, and *I'll try* sounds like a hedge. Neither says how settled the speaker is.

| Form | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| `thehul` / `thehum` / `thehur` | decided for good / decided, can still change / decided for now |
| `thudul` / `thudum` / `thudur` | committed to trying / tries / trial run (failing is useful to know) |

> I'm going vegetarian thudur.  
> We're moving to Denver thehul.  
> I'm taking the job thehum.  
> I'm learning piano thudul.  
> We're skipping the party thehur.

**Why here:** a trial run lowers the stakes of starting something, which fits the empowerment theme. **Sources** (editors): [decision](../grammar/intention.md#decision), [try](../grammar/intention.md#try).

### Lesson 8: Feelings in three parts

**Gap:** one English emotion word (*anxious*, *resentful*, *proud*) hides three things: which need is at stake, where the feeling sits or what it points at, and how it moves. A single label can be a judgment in disguise.

Build on the thanks and sorry words from Lesson 5: `th` + sake + `tha` (met) or `thu` (unmet) + time ending. Keep that whole word, then add a **locus** and a **motion ending**. Put it after the English clause the feeling is about, like any stance drop-in. On its own, with no clause, it says how you feel right now.

Show the parts every time, because the word now carries two **-l / -m / -r** slots: the time ending from Lesson 5 stays in the middle, and the motion ending goes last.

| `thulo` | `thu` | `r` | `ao` | `r` |
|---------|-------|-----|------|-----|
| competence | unmet | for now (time) | over the room (locus) | surging (motion) |

| Locus | Meaning |
|-------|---------|
| `a` | mine, held inside |
| `ao` | the atmosphere of the situation |
| `uo` | I can't place where it comes from |
| `o` | aimed at someone |
| `oe` | turning to someone for comfort |
| `ue` | pushing back |
| `e` | felt on someone's behalf |
| `u` | moving off (avoiding, hiding) |
| `ua` | out of fight: giving up |
| `ae` | using yourself to placate |

| Motion | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| **-l** | still, muted, numb |
| **-m** | flowing, steady |
| **-r** | surging, in waves |

The motion endings are the settled-to-passing scale from earlier lessons, applied to water: still water is lasting (**-l**), a current is the default (**-m**), and a wave is right now (**-r**). Motion is not strength: steady anger is flowing.

> We shipped the draft thulothamar. (proud: competence met, inside, surging)  
> The review is tomorrow thulothuraor. (anxious: competence at stake for now, over the room, surging)  
> He rewrote my memo thahuthumuem. (objecting: autonomy unmet, pushing back, flowing)  
> My sister got laid off thahuthulem. (upset for her: autonomy unmet for good, on her behalf, flowing)  
> Thulothuruol, and I can't say why. (uneasy: competence at stake for now, unplaced, still)

**Why here:** this is the richest compassion and self-understanding tool, and it's great for journaling. It comes late because it combines the sakes from Lesson 5 with the ending logic from earlier lessons, and it gives learners the most to build. Present the locus and motion tables as a menu, not something to memorize. **Source** (editors): [emotion compose](../grammar/sakes.md#emotion-compose).

### Closing: learn the full language

A short final page, not a lesson. It ends Claritish and is the only place that links into the grammar.

- Recap what the learner can now say: *could be*, sources, labels, needs, consent, decisions, feelings.
- Show that these were never add-ons. Each drop-in is a real Agazan word, and the full language uses them the same way inside sentences built for them. Show one full sentence that stacks several of them, with a morph line.
- Show what Claritish can't do. In English, these distinctions appear only when the speaker remembers to add them. Agazan is built around them, so they are there every time you speak, write or think in it.
- **Plug:** one clear call to action, *Start learning Agazan*, linking to the start of the learning path ([introduction](../grammar/introduction.md#how-to-learn)).
