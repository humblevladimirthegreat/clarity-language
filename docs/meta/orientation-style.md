# Orientation page wording and voice

Wording rules for editors who write the two orientation pages: [Why Ageben](../grammar/purpose.md) (`purpose.md`) and [Ageben Introduction](../grammar/introduction.md) (`introduction.md`). What each page owns is in [what belongs where](grammar-docs.md#what-belongs-where).

[doc-style.md](doc-style.md) applies here, and its build checks run on both pages. One group of its rules is replaced. The lead rules under [explain before you slogan](doc-style.md#explain-before-you-slogan) were written for a section that teaches a form, and an orientation page teaches none: [the feature entry](#feature-entry) does that job on the tour. [Which rules carry over](#carry-over) lists the rest.

This page adds only what is different. It is private editor guidance, and grammar pages never link to it or mention it.

| Rule | Section |
|------|---------|

| A tour entry says what English blurs, what Ageben marks, and what that helps you do, and stops | [The feature entry](#feature-entry) |
| A spelled form appears only where it is fully glossed | [Forms and notation](#forms-notation) |
| No capitalized gloss labels and no cue notation | [Forms and notation](#forms-notation) |

| Bold follows the stage-page rule, with one exception in Limits | [Bold](#bold) |
| Each part of the introduction keeps its own register | [Three registers](#three-registers) |

Run the [closing tests](#before-you-finish) on anything you wrote or rewrote.

## The reader
<a id="reader"></a>

The reader is a visitor deciding whether Ageben is worth their time. They know no Ageben, have ordinary school grammar, and may have arrived on this page from a link. They have not read [How to learn](../grammar/introduction.md#how-to-learn), so nothing that page defines (the table columns, cues, the level tags) can be assumed.

Two things follow:

- Every term is glossed in the sentence that first uses it, on each of the two pages separately. A gloss on one page does not cover the other.
- The page is judged by whether the reader can tell what the language does for them, not by whether they could produce a form afterward. Detail that only helps someone produce a form belongs on the linked page.

## Calibrated claims
<a id="calibrated-claims"></a>

[Limits](../grammar/purpose.md#limits) says the benefits are a design intent and not a proven result. Every other sentence on both pages stays inside that. Ageben is a language for marking how you know something and how strongly, so a page that overclaims for it contradicts what it is describing.

| Kind of sentence | Write | Not |
|------------------|-------|-----|
| What a feature does to the sentence | Plain fact: "the ending says whether the cost can change" | A hedge on something that is simply true of the grammar |
| What that does for the speaker | *helps you*, *lets you*, *gives you a chance to*, *is meant to* | *makes you*, *stops you*, *ensures*, *cures* |
| How often it works | Nothing, or an explicit statement that this is the intent | *often*, *usually*, *is enough to*, when no evidence backs the frequency |
| An argument in Criticisms | The conclusion the premises support, with its strength stated | A conclusion stronger than Limits (*therefore Ageben changes how speakers think*) |
| A value judgment | The conditions it depends on, then the judgment as the author's view | *clearly*, *obviously*, *it is morally good* as bare fact |

**Test (claims):** read the sentence beside Limits. If a skeptical reader could quote the two together as a contradiction, weaken the sentence.

The grammar facts need no softening. The split is between what the form marks, which is certain, and what marking it does for a person, which is the intent.

## The feature entry
<a id="feature-entry"></a>

The tour on Why Ageben is a list of entries, one per feature. An entry has three beats, each a short paragraph of one to three sentences:

1. **What English blurs.** One everyday English sentence or word, and the readings it runs together.
2. **What Ageben marks.** The choice the speaker makes, named in English, with a link to the section that teaches it.
3. **What that helps you do.** The human [consequence](doc-style.md#consequence), calibrated as [above](#calibrated-claims).

Then the entry stops. Where a Claritish lesson covers the feature, one trailer line follows: "In English:" and the lesson link. Every entry that has a lesson carries the trailer, and no entry carries any other kind of trailer.

What stays out of an entry:

| Smell | Why it fails | Prefer |
|-------|--------------|--------|
| Where the word goes, or what it attaches to | The reader cannot use placement yet | Cut. The linked section teaches it |
| A side distinction (*it is also not the same as…*) | A second feature inside the first one's entry | Its own entry, or nothing |
| A list of every value the choice can take | Inventory, which is the grammar's job | Two or three values that show the range, then the link |
| Advice in the imperative (*prefer numbered alternatives*) | Describes a habit, where the tour describes the language | Say what the grammar offers. If the grammar offers nothing here, the entry does not belong on the tour |
| A fourth paragraph | The entry has become a lesson | Move the extra to the owning section |

An entry is about something the grammar or vocabulary does. A good practice that any language could follow is not a feature.

The groups above the entries (an aim, then a thinking trap) each get a short opener. An aim's opener says what the aim is, in a full sentence about the language. A trap's opener says what goes wrong in thinking. Neither previews the entries one by one ([one owner per topic](#one-owner)).

## Forms and notation
<a id="forms-notation"></a>

A spelled Ageben form appears on these pages only where the page fully glosses it: the worked sentence in [Ageben at a glance](../grammar/purpose.md#at-a-glance), the one Claritish sample, and the composition of the name. Tour entries name the choice in English and link out. A bare form in an entry gives this reader nothing to do with it and suggests the tour is a lesson.

Three kinds of notation belong to the grammar pages and are not used here:

| Notation | Why not | Instead |
|----------|---------|---------|
| Capitalized gloss labels (the grammar's feature names written in capitals) | Capitals are morph gloss notation, which this reader has not met | The grammar's English name in lowercase, glossed in the same sentence. Keep the name the same as the grammar's, so the link lands on a heading the reader recognizes ([use the grammar's terms](claritish-style.md#grammar-terms)) |
| `(cue: …)` | Defined in How to learn, which comes later | Say the picture in a sentence after the plain claim, or leave it to the conceptual metaphors list |
| Slashed role letters outside the at-a-glance walkthrough | Phonology notation | "the first letter", "the ending" |

The introduction's structural sections follow the same rule. They describe a design choice in English (every content word starts with a letter that names its role) and leave spellings to the stage pages.

## One owner per topic
<a id="one-owner"></a>

The two pages overlap by nature, so each topic has one owner. The other page gives it one sentence and a link.

| Topic | Owner |
|-------|-------|
| Psychological goals, the feature tour, the worked example | Why Ageben |
| Limits, criticisms | Why Ageben |
| The name | Introduction |
| Structural goals, and the tools they make possible | Introduction |
| Reading order, sidebar groups, table columns | Introduction, under How to learn |
| License, acknowledgments | Introduction |
| What Claritish is | The Claritish track. Each page mentions it once in the body, and Why Ageben may repeat it in Next |

Inside Why Ageben, a feature is previewed at most once before its entry. The worked example may show a feature in action, since showing is not explaining. After that, either the aim's opener or the trap's opener may point at it, not both, and neither explains it.

A term's gloss works the same way. *Sake* is glossed where it first appears and not again lower down. When sections move, move the gloss to the new first use.

Order follows dependence: a section that states the premise of another comes before it. The argument for why wording can help at all is the premise of the tour.

## Voice
<a id="voice"></a>

- **Person.** Second person for the reader. Sentences that describe the language have Ageben, a feature, or a word as their subject: "Ageben marks…", "the ending says…".
- **No spec fragments.** An opener such as "Support self-acceptance and make judgments visible" is a design brief addressed to nobody. Write the full sentence: what the aim is and what the language does toward it.
- **Author *I*.** Allowed in Criticisms and Acknowledgments, which are signed opinion and thanks. It stays out of the purpose statement, the worked example, the tour, Limits, and everything on the introduction above the license. Maintainer *we* is not used anywhere.
- **Objections.** In Criticisms, state the objection at full strength in its own paragraph before answering it. The answer is the author's view and says so.
- **English as the contrast.** English is the comparison because it is the reader's language. Say what it leaves unmarked, not that it is defective. The same rule holds for Claritish cards ([card copy](claritish-style.md#card-copy)).

## Bold
<a id="bold"></a>

The stage-page rule holds: bold a term at the point it is defined and a control's label, and nothing for emphasis. Stress on an ordinary word (*when*, *not*, *only*) is carried by word order or by italics for a quoted English word.

One exception: the sentence in Limits that says Ageben is not therapy, counseling, or medical advice may be bold, because a reader skimming for exactly that must find it. It is the only bold disclaimer on the page.

In How to learn, the sidebar group names and column names are labels and take bold as controls do. A whole sentence in bold is emphasis and is not allowed.

## Three registers on the introduction
<a id="three-registers"></a>

The introduction does three jobs, and each has its own register. Keep them from leaking into each other.

| Part | Register | Rules |
|------|----------|-------|
| The name, purpose, structural goals, tools | Overview | Everything on this page. Headings are plain claims a visitor can read alone (a specialist adjective such as *referential* is not one). One new term per sentence, as in [plain language](doc-style.md#plain-language-no-assumed-linguistics) |
| How to learn, table columns | Site manual | [Interface instructions](doc-style.md#interface-instructions): the steps in order in one short paragraph, then the sidebar groups and tools in a table, each with what it is for and when to open it. Say each instruction once. This section defines the column names for every other page, so each definition must read alone |
| License, acknowledgments | Terms and thanks | License wording is precise before it is friendly, and may be third person. It still follows punctuation and plain-language rules where precision allows. Acknowledgments are author *I*, and vary their sentence openings |

## Before you finish
<a id="before-you-finish"></a>

1. **Cold start.** Read the section as someone who landed on it from a search. Does every term, label, and piece of notation explain itself there?
2. **Limits beside it.** Put each benefit sentence next to Limits. Does any pair read as a contradiction?
3. **Three beats.** For each tour entry, can you point to what English blurs, what Ageben marks, and what that helps you do? Is anything left over? Cut it or move it to the owning section.
4. **Could they use it.** Is there a form, placement rule, or inventory the reader cannot act on yet? Replace it with the choice in English and the link.
5. **Once.** Search both pages for the feature's key word. Is it explained in one place and previewed at most once before that?
6. **Who is speaking.** Is every sentence either about the language, addressed to the reader, or signed *I* in a section that allows it?
