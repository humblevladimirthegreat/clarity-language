# Claritish wording and voice

Wording policies for editors writing the **Claritish** track under **`docs/grammar/claritish/`**. What the track is, the lesson shape, what counts as a drop-in, placement, and the link rules: [Claritish track](grammar-docs.md#claritish-track).

[doc-style.md](doc-style.md) applies here unchanged: register, second person, punctuation over em dashes, one idea per heading, bold sparingly, Agebon in backticks, [plain language](doc-style.md#plain-language-no-assumed-linguistics). Claritish is the same voice aimed at a reader who has not opened the grammar and may never. This page adds only what is different, and owns the [hyphenated English host](#hyphenated-host), which exists in Claritish and nowhere in Agebon.

## Use the grammar's terms
<a id="grammar-terms"></a>

Name the feature the way the grammar names it. The reader is learning real Agebon words, so the English name they learn here must be the one they will meet again: **label scope** and **scope vowel**, **evidential channel**, **emotion locus** and **emotion motion**, **stance** word, **sake**, **tone mark**, **permission** / **requirement** / **consent**, **decision**, **try**. [terminology.md](../grammar/terminology.md) is the list; do not link to it from a lesson.

Do **not** coin a Claritish-only word for a feature that already has an English name. A track-local synonym costs the reader the term twice and makes the lesson stop transferring at the handoff.

| Smell | Prefer |
|-------|--------|
| *reach*, for how far a label applies | *label scope*; the vowel is the *scope vowel* |
| *channel*, introduced as if it were a Claritish word | *evidential channel* (*how you know*), then *channel* for short |
| *place* and *motion*, for a composed feeling | *emotion locus* and *emotion motion* |

Gloss the term in plain English in the **same sentence as first use**, exactly as [doc-style](doc-style.md#plain-language-no-assumed-linguistics) requires: *the scope vowel, which says how far the label reaches*. Using the grammar's name is not a licence to leave it unglossed.

Two terms the track owns, because the grammar has no name for them: **drop-in** (an Agebon word used inside an English sentence) and the **gap** section heading. Add no others.

**Naming a feature is not teaching its grammar.** Keep out the machinery the reader cannot use yet: role letters, hosting, slots and word order, joins, spans, citation forms, the concrete / abstract split. A lesson says what the word means and where it goes in the English sentence, and stops.

## The gap names an English failure
<a id="the-gap"></a>

`## The gap` is two or three sentences on the English habit that misleads, with the real English words in italics (*I think* hides a guess; *he's a liar* turns one act into someone's nature). Agebon enters only in the last sentence, as one clause saying what it names instead.

The failure is psychological, not structural. A gap is a thinking trap the reader will recognize from their own week: the guess that sounds like a fact, the rumor that sounds like a sighting, the one act that becomes a nature. "English lacks a word for X" is not a gap on its own.

| Smell | Why it fails | Prefer |
|-------|--------------|--------|
| Opens with the Agebon form or inventory | Nothing yet says why the reader wants it | Name what English loses first; the forms come under `## The forms` |
| "Agebon marks evidentiality." | A feature announcement; no trap, and an unglossed term | "*He said it's fixed* and *I saw it's fixed* sound the same once you repeat them." |
| A gap that is only tidiness (*English is inconsistent here*) | Gives the reader no reason to change how they talk | Name the cost: what gets believed, or who gets blamed |

## Forms, then placement, then endings
<a id="forms"></a>

`## The forms` leads with the small table, then one sentence on where the drop-in goes in the English sentence, then the endings.

- **Give a default, and an escape.** Say which form to use when the reader cannot tell which fits ("when you are not sure which ending fits, use `thomam`").
- **Long menus say they are menus.** A locus or channel inventory is followed by *you don't need to memorize it*.
- **Say when to leave it out.** Every lesson marks at least one place the drop-in is unnecessary, and none of them implies that plain English is a mistake: *none of the three is the "right" one*; *leave the suffix off when the scope doesn't matter*.
- **Rule out the near miss.** Where the obvious English reading is wrong, deny it in one short sentence (*motion is not strength*).
- **Endings** are the settled-to-passing scale, with the cue **-l ≈ lasting, -r ≈ right now**, and each lesson notes when the scale comes back. Allowed, required, agreed grades the source instead, as do the prescription endings in Oughts and motives (invited / offered / trial). Labels and Can and can't teach hyphenated suffixes, which take no ending.
- **Pronunciation** is taught once, in How sure are you?, in English keywords. Later lessons do not repeat it.

## The hyphenated English host
<a id="hyphenated-host"></a>

Claritish adds to an English word in two places: [label scope](../grammar/predication.md#label-scope), English word + `-th` + scope vowel (`lied-tha`), and [ability](../grammar/intention.md#ability-changeability), English verb + `-x` + vowel (`sing-xu`). The hyphen marks an English host and the letters after it are spelled exactly as Agebon spells them. Neither suffix takes an ending, because its vowel carries the meaning.

The hyphen belongs to Claritish only. Agebon writes these mid-word on an Agebon root with no hyphen (`valamuthal`, `vezuyoxal`; the ending is the one the Agebon word would take), so a lesson teaching a suffix never implies the hyphen survives into the full language.

**Marking:** a hyphenated English host (`lied-tha`) is an English span, so the build does not parse it. Table rows for a suffix use `-tha` or `-xa`, not `…-tha` (a template that does not parse). A tone mark attaches to the English word it colors and is written bare (`%You're the worst.`), not as a backtick span.

## Examples and practice
<a id="examples-practice"></a>

About five one-line examples in a single blockquote, two trailing spaces per line. They are ordinary adult situations (a shipped draft, a delayed flight, a rewritten memo, a sister laid off), not toy sentences, and they carry no Agebon beyond the drop-in. The [house cast](grammar-docs.md#house-cast) stays out of the lessons; names appear only in the full sentence on the closing page.

After the block, one short paragraph unpacks two or three of the examples, not all five, and picks the ones where the ending or the scope does the work.

Practice items give the English sentence in italics, then a parenthesized note saying **what the speaker means**, never which form to write ("(could be; you'll call and check)", not "(use `thomal`)"). A note may name the parts when the word is composed, as in Wants and plans. Answers go in `::: details Show answer`.

## No Agebon sentences, no glosses
<a id="no-glosses"></a>

Lessons carry no full Agebon sentences and no morph gloss lines. Explain a drop-in by what it means, never by cutting it into Agebon morphemes. Where a word is built from parts, label the parts in plain English in a table (the need / locus / motion row), and that is the ceiling. The single glossed Agebon sentence on the closing page is the only one in the track.

## Back-references
<a id="back-references"></a>

Lessons carry no numbers. A lesson may name an earlier lesson by title, in plain text, when it reuses its form ("start from a thanks or sorry word from Thanks and sorry that say why"), as a reminder of one word, not a recap. No links between lessons, no links into the grammar, and no previews of lessons ahead outside the list on the intro page.

## Editor cues and cards
<a id="editor-cards"></a>

The [journal editor](grammar-docs.md#claritish-track) is for private journaling: the writer talking to themselves. Its cues and card questions are hand-written data, its forms are not.

**Cues** (`data/claritish-triggers.csv`, one row per English habit):

- `cue`: patterns separated by ` | `. Words are space-separated; `a/b` is a choice of words, `(…)` an optional word, `*` any letters inside a word (`*ing`), `@list` any word of a list in `data/claritish-words.csv` (`@trait`, `(@degree)`), and punctuation is its own word (`sorry ?`, `; )`, `can't ./,/!`). A contraction is one word, so write `I'm sorry | I am sorry`, not `I'm/I am sorry`.
- `exclude`: patterns for the common uses that are not the trap (*I think so*, *thanks to the rain*, *sorry to hear*). An exclusion that overlaps a cue silences it.
- `flow` and `defaults`: the question flow, and any answer the cue already implies (`channel=reported` for *I heard*).
- `placement`: `after-clause`, `after-noun`, `suffix` (a hyphen suffix on the English verb after the cue, which replaces the cue: *I can't sing* → `sing-xu`), `on-cue` (a hyphen suffix on the cue's last word, which stays: *he's a liar* → `liar-the`), or `before-word` (a tone mark at the start of the sentence). `join` (`space`, `comma`, or `sentence` for a drop-in that follows the sentence, as the thanks and sorry examples do), and `drop_cue` (`yes` when the lesson's examples leave the English cue out).
- `gap_sentence`: which sentence of the lesson's `## The gap` the card shows, so the trap is never written twice.
- `mirror`: the mirror panel's name for the habit, one and many, split by `/` (`unmarked guess/unmarked guesses`). Rows for the same habit share it. It describes what is left in English, never a fault: no *missing*, no *should*.
- `exempt`: a reason, for an italic gap phrase that is not a cue (the drop-in's own meaning). Such a row raises nothing.

Every italic phrase in a cued lesson's gap must raise one of that lesson's cues or match an exempt row; the build checks it. A cue with no fitting drop-in is not a cue: it goes to the grammar gap process, not into the editor.

**Word lists** (`data/claritish-words.csv`, one `list,word` row per word) hold the open sets a cue cannot spell out: the negative trait words a label names (`trait`), the emotion words a feeling names (`emotion-unmet`, `emotion-met`), and the words between a subject and the label (`degree`: *so*, *such*, *a bit*), and the people a label can be about besides a pronoun (`person`: *boss*, *dad*, *friends*). An optional third column, `preset`, on an emotion word names the feelings composer's starting answer (`sake=understanding` for *confused*); it names a choice of the feeling flow, never a spelling, and only where the need is clear. Each word is one lowercase word and sits in one list; the build checks both. Leave out a word another lesson's cue already owns (*grateful* is a thanks) and a word that is mostly not about a person or a feeling (*stuck*, *down*, *lost*).

- **Labels** mark a negative trait word after a subject and a copula (*he's a liar*, *I was so lazy*, *my boss is a liar*; the trait list has the plural nouns too, *they're idiots*), and someone else's painful emotion word in the present tense (*he's anxious*), which calls a feeling a type. No praise words: the lesson's gap is about judgment.
- **Feelings** mark an emotion word only in the first person (*I'm so anxious*, *it made me angry*, *feeling proud*). A feelings drop-in names the writer's own feeling, so someone else's (*my sister was upset*) has no drop-in to offer. The emotion word stays in the sentence.

**Flows** (`data/claritish-flows.json`): each question's answers name a place on the [cheat sheet](../grammar/claritish/cheat-sheet.md), never a spelling: a row label, a column header (`-l`), a cell by its English (`autonomy`) or by the meaning in the cell beside it (`can never` for `-xu`), or a prose line (the `Sake word:` formula). `{question}` in a reference is filled with the answer. A choice with no `label` takes the sheet's own English; a question's `labelFrom` reads labels that depend on an earlier answer (*tolerated* for allowed, *by custom* for required).

- `commands`: the slash commands that open the flow with no English cue (`/sure`), each with any answers it presets (`"plan": { "kind": "planned" }`). A command puts its drop-in where it was typed: a suffix joins the word before it, a tone mark the word after it.
- A form with `"oneWord": true` joins its parts with nothing (a feeling: sake word + locus + motion), not a space (`thawothum blawan`); `"breakdown": true` shows the word part by part, each part over the answer that chose it, as the cheat sheet's feelings table does. A prose line that lists endings (`Motion ending: **-l** still, …`) is read one entry at a time with `item` (`"item": "{motion}"`).
- A choice with `"requires": "command"` is offered only from a command (*can*, which no English cue asks for). A choice with `none` ends in no drop-in: the card shows its line and offers only *Leave it in English*, for a lesson that says when to leave the word off.

**Reading back** needs no data of its own. Hover glosses and near misses (`thovom` → `thomam`) come from the forms the flows produce. English words that come close to a form (*thunder*, *than*) are found at build from the English helper's WordNet index and compromise's lexicon, so they are never flagged and never open autocomplete; a new form brings its own.

**Card copy:** questions ask what the writer means (*What will you do about the guess?*), with the lesson's default answer first. Use the grammar's terms as the lessons do, and nothing that implies English is a mistake: no *fix*, *error*, or *should*. *Leave it in English* is a normal outcome.
